## Intuition

Chapter 3 handled the weight precision alpha by picking the single value that maximises
the evidence, an outer optimisation wrapped around the inner posterior fit. Every re-fit
of the weights needs alpha, and every re-estimate of alpha needs the weights, so the
whole thing is solved by alternating the two until neither moves.

That alternation is coordinate ascent whether or not anyone calls it that. Giving alpha a
distribution of its own turns the same alternation into the mean-field procedure from
earlier in this chapter, with no separate evidence function to derive. Both views converge
to the same place, but only one hands over a full posterior over alpha and a bound proving
the fit only ever improved.

Nothing here changes what the model can express. What changes is how much of it survives
contact with the data: a term the data does not need keeps its prior share of the
precision, and the effect looks identical to the pruning of the last section.

## The math

With a Gaussian prior on the weights conditioned on $\alpha$ and a Gamma prior on $\alpha$
itself, the mean-field factors $q(\mathbf{w})q(\alpha)$ each stay in their prior's family:

<MathBlock id="10.93" name="alpha posterior">

$$q^{*}(\alpha) = \mathrm{Gam}(\alpha \mid a_N, b_N)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="10.99" name="weight posterior">

$$q^{*}(\mathbf{w}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{m}_N, \mathbf{S}_N)$$

</MathBlock>

with parameters that reference each other's current expectation rather than a fixed
constant:

<MathBlock id="10.100" name="posterior mean">

$$\mathbf{m}_N = \beta \mathbf{S}_N \boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}} \mathbf{t}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="10.101" name="posterior covariance">

$$\mathbf{S}_N = \big(\mathbb{E}[\alpha]\mathbf{I} + \beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\big)^{-1}$$

</MathBlock>

</Figure>

In the noninformative limit $a_0=b_0=0$ this reduces to a single closed ratio,

<MathBlock id="10.104" name="noninformative expected alpha">

$$\mathbb{E}[\alpha] = \frac{M}{\mathbf{m}_N^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{m}_N + \mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{S}_N)}$$

</MathBlock>

echoing chapter 3's evidence-maximised $\alpha$ without the outer loop that produced it.
Offer more terms than the data needs and the extra weights shrink toward zero directly,
because a bigger $\mathbb{E}[\alpha]$ pulls every coordinate of $\mathbf{m}_N$ down at once
through 10.101.

<Figure caption="Posterior mean weight per polynomial term, order 7 fitted against data generated by a cubic. Terms past the true order (red) sit near zero." source="10.9">
  <PosteriorWeightShrinkage client:visible />
</Figure>

The whole fit is tracked by one number:

<MathBlock id="10.107" name="lower bound">

$$\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}[\ln p(\mathbf{t}, \mathbf{w}, \alpha)] - \mathbb{E}[\ln q(\mathbf{w}, \alpha)]$$

</MathBlock>

The peak never moves off the true order across the whole noise range: the bound is

capacity the data cannot support never wins.

## Worked example

Twelve points generated from a cubic, offered a seventh-order polynomial basis and a
noninformative prior. Coordinate ascent converges $\mathbb{E}[\alpha]$ to a single value
shared by all eight weights, and by 10.101 every coordinate of $\mathbf{S}_N$ shrinks by
the same factor.

The posterior mean weights for terms $x^4$ through $x^7$ land under $0.05$ in magnitude,
against weights above $0.3$ for the true cubic's own terms: a twenty-fold gap that
separates signal from noise without ever comparing the order-3 and order-7 models
directly. The converged lower bound at order 7 sits a fraction below the order-3 bound in
the widget above, exactly the gap 10.107 charges for the four terms carrying no
information.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does variational linear regression need no outer evidence-maximisation loop?" concepts={["variational-linear-regression"]}>
Because putting a distribution on alpha folds its re-estimation into the same
coordinate-ascent cycle that fits the weights (10.93, 10.99), rather than wrapping a
separate optimisation around a fixed-alpha posterior.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does a large offered model order do to the unneeded weights?" concepts={["variational-linear-regression"]}>
Their posterior means shrink toward zero, because a single shared E[alpha] rises to fit
the terms that matter and, through 10.101, pulls every coordinate's variance and mean down
with it.
</Recall>

<Recall question="How does 10.104 relate to chapter 3's evidence approximation?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
It is the same ratio chapter 3 reaches by iterating a fixed-point equation derived from
the evidence function, arrived at here as the noninformative limit of a Gamma posterior's
own mean instead.
</Recall>
