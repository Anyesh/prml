## Intuition

Chapter 9 fit a mixture of Gaussians by alternating two calculations, and that alternation
came from a bound: a function that always sits below the true log-likelihood and touches it
exactly at the current guess. Variational inference is that same trick freed from mixtures.
Any posterior too tangled to write down gets a bound built from a simpler distribution, and
climbing the bound by coordinate ascent stands in for integrating something that has no
closed form.

The price is a choice the reader does not get to skip: which simpler family, and measured
by which notion of closeness. Restrict the approximation to independent pieces and ask it
to minimise one direction of KL divergence, and it under-reports how uncertain it really
is. Ask for the other direction, and it over-reports, spreading mass into regions the truth
never visits. Neither is a bug to be fixed. They are what "independent pieces" costs,
depending on which way you round.

## The math

Any distribution $q(Z)$ over the latent variables decomposes the log evidence exactly:

<MathBlock id="10.2" name="evidence decomposition">

$$\ln p(\mathbf{X}) = \mathcal{L}(q) + \mathrm{KL}(q \| p)$$

</MathBlock>

Since $\mathrm{KL} \geq 0$, $\mathcal{L}(q)$ lower-bounds the evidence for any $q$, and
maximising it over a restricted family is exactly minimising that family's distance to the
true posterior. Restrict $q$ to factorise, $q(\mathbf{Z}) = \prod_i q_i(Z_i)$, and calculus
of variations gives the optimal factor in closed form:

<MathBlock id="10.9" name="optimal factor">

$$\ln q_j^\star(Z_j) = \mathbb{E}_{i \neq j}[\ln p(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Z})] + \text{const}$$

</MathBlock>

Each factor depends on every other through an expectation, so the factors are solved by
coordinate ascent, not in one pass. For a correlated bivariate Gaussian split into
$q_1(z_1)q_2(z_2)$, this gives

<MathBlock id="10.12" name="forward-KL factor form">

$$q^\star(z_1) = \mathcal{N}(z_1 \mid m_1, \Lambda_{11}^{-1})$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="10.15" name="forward-KL mean update">

$$m_1 = \mu_1 - \Lambda_{11}^{-1}\Lambda_{12}(\mathbb{E}[z_2] - \mu_2)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Ten sweeps from a deliberately wrong start. The path lands exactly on the true mean regardless of where it began, because that mean is the only fixed point." source="10.2">
  <CoordinateAscentTrajectory client:visible />
</Figure>

with $q_2$ symmetric. The fixed point is always $\mathbb{E}[z_1]=\mu_1$, $\mathbb{E}[z_2]=\mu_2$,
but each factor's variance is the *conditional* one, $1/\Lambda_{11}$, smaller than the true
marginal whenever the axes are correlated. Minimising the reverse KL instead has a
one-line closed form, no iteration required:

<MathBlock id="10.17" name="reverse-KL closed form">

$$q_j^\star(Z_j) = p(Z_j)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Same true Gaussian, same three variances compared. Forward KL always reports the smallest; reverse KL always reports the true one, however elongated the ellipse gets." width="wide">
  <VarianceUnderreporting client:visible />
</Figure>

each factor becomes the true marginal, always at least as wide. On a bimodal target,
forward KL locks onto whichever mode it starts nearest, while reverse KL spreads across
both, mass and all, even into the gap between them:

<Figure caption="A bimodal target, two forward-KL local minima (one per mode), and the single reverse-KL fit spanning both. Reverse KL puts real mass where the truth has none.">
  <BimodalKlComparison client:visible />
</Figure>

The same machinery applied to a Gaussian's unknown mean and precision gives an iterative
pair of updates:

<MathBlock id="10.26" name="univariate mean update">

$$\mu_N = \frac{\lambda_0 \mu_0 + N\bar{x}}{\lambda_0 + N}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="10.29" name="univariate precision shape">

$$a_N = a_0 + \frac{N}{2}$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

Take $\alpha_0 = a_0 = b_0 = \lambda_0 = 0$, the noninformative prior, on fifteen points drawn
from $\mathcal{N}(2.0, 1.3^2)$. Coordinate ascent has no prior term to fight, so $\mu_N$
equals the sample mean on the very first sweep and never moves again; only $\mathbb{E}[\tau]$
still has to settle.

<Figure caption="Eight sweeps. The mean line is flat from sweep one; the precision line still climbs toward its fixed point." source="10.4">
  <UnivariateGaussianVbConvergence client:visible />
</Figure>

At convergence $\mathbb{E}[\tau]$ solves to exactly $1/s^2$ with $s^2$ the *unbiased* sample
variance, dividing by $N-1$ rather than $N$: the one place in this section where the
variational answer matches a familiar classical estimator exactly, not approximately.

## Recall

<Recall question="What does restricting q to factorise buy, and what does it cost?" concepts={["mean-field-approximation"]}>
It buys a closed-form update per factor from 10.9, turning an intractable integral into
coordinate ascent. It costs correlation: the approximation cannot represent any dependence
between the factors it has split apart.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does minimising KL(q||p) under-report variance while KL(p||q) does not?" concepts={["kl-divergence-asymmetry"]}>
KL(q||p) penalises q for placing mass where p is small, so q retreats into whichever region
p is largest and reports that region's narrower conditional spread. KL(p||q) penalises the
opposite: q must cover everywhere p has mass, so it reports the true, wider marginal.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is L(q) called a lower bound rather than an approximation to ln p(X)?" concepts={["variational-lower-bound"]}>
Because 10.2 holds as an exact identity for any q: L(q) plus a non-negative KL term equals
ln p(X) exactly. L(q) never exceeds the evidence, and it equals the evidence only when q is
the true posterior.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does the reverse-KL update need no iteration when the forward-KL one does?" concepts={["kl-divergence-asymmetry"]}>
The reverse-KL optimum is each factor's true marginal, 10.17, which does not depend on the
other factor's current guess at all. The forward-KL optimum couples every factor to every
other through an expectation, so it must be solved by coordinate ascent.
</Recall>
