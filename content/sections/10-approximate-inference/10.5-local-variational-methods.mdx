## Intuition

Every bound so far has been global: a whole distribution standing in for another whole
distribution, everywhere at once. Some functions resist that. The logistic sigmoid inside
a Bernoulli likelihood has no Gaussian conjugate anywhere, global or otherwise, so
averaging over a posterior through it has no closed form to reach for.

A local bound gives up global accuracy on purpose. Instead of one approximation good
everywhere, build a family of simple curves, each exact at exactly one point and safely on
one side everywhere else. Pick the point that matters for the calculation at hand, and the
approximation is no longer an approximation there.

The price is a new number to choose: which point. That choice becomes one more thing the
overall fit optimises, alongside whatever else it was already solving for, and the two
kinds of optimisation turn out to interleave into the same coordinate ascent already
familiar from every other section here.

## The math

A convex function lies above every one of its tangent lines, so it can be rebuilt as a
maximum over them:

<MathBlock id="10.125" name="tangent-line bound">

$$y(x) = f(\xi) + f'(\xi)(x - \xi), \qquad f(x) = \max_{\xi} \, y(x)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="exp(-x) and three of its tangent lines. Each is a valid lower bound, exact only where it touches." source="10.10">
  <ConvexDualityTangent client:visible />
</Figure>

The logistic sigmoid is not itself convex, but its logarithm is, which gives an upper
bound on the sigmoid through the same duality:

<MathBlock id="10.136" name="convex-duality upper bound">

$$\ln \sigma(x) \le \lambda x - g(\lambda)$$

</MathBlock>

Applying the same trick to $x^2$ instead of $x$ produces a bound that is concave rather
than convex in the right variable, and a specific coefficient falls out of matching slopes
at the touch point:

<MathBlock id="10.141" name="lambda of xi">

$$\lambda(\xi) = \frac{1}{2\xi}\Big[\sigma(\xi) - \tfrac12\Big]$$

</MathBlock>

which assembles into a Gaussian-shaped lower bound on the sigmoid itself:

<MathBlock id="10.144" name="Gaussian lower bound on the sigmoid">

$$\sigma(x) \ge \sigma(\xi) \exp\!\left[\frac{x - \xi}{2} - \lambda(\xi)(x^2 - \xi^2)\right]$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The exponential upper bound (10.137) and the Gaussian lower bound (10.144) sandwiching the same sigmoid, from opposite sides and different derivations.">
  <UpperVsLowerBound client:visible />
</Figure>

Exact at $x=\xi$ and $x=-\xi$ by construction, this is the bound that turns a Bernoulli
likelihood into something Gaussian integration can handle.

## Worked example

At $\xi = 2.5$, $\lambda(\xi) = [\sigma(2.5) - 0.5]/5 \approx 0.0842$. The bound
$\sigma(\xi)\exp[(x-\xi)/2 - \lambda(\xi)(x^2-\xi^2)]$ evaluates to $\sigma(2.5) \approx
0.924$ at $x=2.5$ exactly, matching the true sigmoid to machine precision, and to
$\sigma(-2.5)\approx 0.076$ at $x=-2.5$, also exact.

At $x=0$, midway between the touch points, the bound gives $\sigma(\xi)\exp[-\xi/2 +
\lambda(\xi)\xi^2] \approx 0.437$, against the true $\sigma(0)=0.5$: an 87-thousandths gap,
the largest anywhere on this curve, sitting exactly where the two touch points are
furthest from doing any work.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the sigmoid need a local rather than a global bound?" concepts={["local-variational-bound"]}>
It has no conjugate Gaussian form anywhere, so no single tractable family approximates it
everywhere at once. A local bound instead buys exactness at one chosen point and accepts
looseness elsewhere.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the variational parameter xi actually choose?" concepts={["local-variational-bound"]}>
The point where the bound touches the true sigmoid exactly, by 10.144; moving xi slides
that touch point, and optimising xi is choosing where the approximation should be tight.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does the bound get looser away from x = +/- xi?" concepts={["local-variational-bound"]}>
It is built from a fixed quadratic shape whose only freedom is where it touches; away from
that point the true sigmoid's curvature and the bound's fixed curvature diverge, by
construction rather than by accident.
</Recall>
