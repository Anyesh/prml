## Intuition

Everyday geometric sense comes from living in three dimensions, and it stops applying
almost immediately past that. Divide a line into ten bins and you need ten cells. Do the
same to a hundred-dimensional cube and the cell count is ten to the hundredth: more cells
than could ever hold one data point each, however much data exists.

The strangeness runs deeper than counting cells. A ball's volume, and a Gaussian's
probability mass, both stop clustering near the centre as dimension grows and instead
crowd into a thin shell near the surface, or into corners past any inscribed sphere. A
neighbourhood that felt local and reliable at low dimension is neither, once the space
around it has enough directions to hide in.

None of this rules out working in high dimensions. It rules out doing it the way low
dimensions taught, covering the whole space with a fixed grid or a fixed set of functions
and hoping density holds up.

## The math

A general polynomial to order 3 in $D$ inputs already carries a triple sum:

<MathBlock id="1.74" name="higher-order polynomial in D dimensions">

$$y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}) = w_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{D}w_ix_i + \sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{j=1}^{D}w_{ij}x_ix_j + \sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\sum_{k=1}^{D}w_{ijk}x_ix_jx_k$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Independent coefficients needed for a degree-M polynomial, by input dimension D. Log scale; a straight line here is already exponential-feeling growth." width="wide">
  <PolynomialParameterGrowth client:visible />
</Figure>

A sphere's volume scales as $r^D$:

<MathBlock id="1.75" name="sphere volume scaling">

$$V_D(r) = K_D r^D$$

</MathBlock>

so the fraction sitting within $\epsilon$ of the surface is dimension-dependent:

<MathBlock id="1.76" name="volume fraction in an outer shell">

$$\frac{V_D(1) - V_D(1-\epsilon)}{V_D(1)} = 1 - (1-\epsilon)^D$$

</MathBlock>

The same concentration happens to probability mass: a Gaussian's radial density moves its
peak outward from the origin as $D$ grows.

<Figure caption="Density against radius, several dimensions, same variance. The peak leaves the origin behind." source="1.23">

</Figure>

## Worked example

A cubic polynomial ($M=3$) needs $286$ independent coefficients at $D=10$ and $176{,}851$
at $D=100$, from the exact count $(D+M)!/(D!\,M!)$: no exponential term anywhere, and
already impractical.

<Figure caption="Ratio of an inscribed sphere's volume to its enclosing cube's, by dimension: computed via the surface-area formula, not estimated." width="wide">
  <SphereInCubeCorners client:visible />
</Figure>

The sphere-in-cube ratio falls from $0.16$ at $D=5$ to about $2.5\times10^{-8}$ at $D=20$.
A sphere touching every face of a cube contains almost none of that cube's volume once $D$
passes a couple of dozen: the volume is in the corners, which stretch into long spikes a
low-dimensional eye never learns to expect.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does a fixed grid over the input space fail in high dimensions?" concepts={["curse-of-dimensionality"]}>
The number of cells grows exponentially with dimension, so the data needed to keep even
most cells non-empty grows exponentially too, far outpacing any realistic dataset.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Where does most of a high-dimensional sphere's volume actually sit?" concepts={["curse-of-dimensionality"]}>
In a thin shell near the surface, not near the centre. The fraction within epsilon of the
surface, 1-(1-epsilon)^D, approaches 1 for any fixed epsilon as D grows.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Does the curse of dimensionality make high-dimensional problems hopeless?" concepts={["data-manifold"]}>
No. Real data rarely fills its ambient space; it clusters near a lower-dimensional
manifold, and exploiting that structure, rather than covering the whole space, is what
makes high-dimensional problems tractable.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does a sphere inscribed in a cube hold almost none of the cube's volume at high D?" concepts={["curse-of-dimensionality"]}>
The ratio falls exponentially in D. Nearly all the cube's volume sits past the sphere, in
its corners, which is the same concentration-away-from-the-centre phenomenon as the shell
result, viewed from outside the sphere instead of inside it.
</Recall>
