## Intuition

Learning that a certain event happened is worth more the less you expected it. That single
idea, degree of surprise, turns out to force an exact mathematical form once one more
condition is added: the surprise from two unrelated events should just add up.

Averaging surprise over a distribution gives a number for how hard that distribution is,
in a very concrete sense, to describe: it is the shortest average code length a sender
could get away with. A distribution concentrated on a few outcomes needs a short average
code, because a good code spends few bits on what actually happens most. A distribution
spread evenly is the hardest case, and there the fair coin, needing its full bit every
time, sets the ceiling.

The rest of this section is that one number reused three ways: for one variable, for the
cost of guessing its distribution wrong, and for how much one variable reveals about
another.

## The math

Requiring $h(x,y)=h(x)+h(y)$ whenever $x,y$ are independent forces a logarithm:

<MathBlock id="1.92" name="information content">

$$h(x) = -\log_2 p(x)$$

</MathBlock>

Its average is entropy, reached equivalently by counting arrangements of $N$ objects into
bins and taking a scaled log of the count:

<MathBlock id="1.94" name="multiplicity">

$$W = \frac{N!}{\prod_i n_i!}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="log(multiplicity) per object converges to the entropy of the bin fractions as N grows, exactly as Stirling's approximation predicts." width="wide">
  <MultiplicityAndEntropy client:visible />
</Figure>

<MathBlock id="1.98" name="entropy">

$$H[p] = -\sum_i p(x_i)\ln p(x_i)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Uniform beats every non-uniform distribution over the same number of states." width="wide">
  <EntropyAcrossDistributions client:visible />
</Figure>

Continuous variables get an analogous but distinct quantity, differential entropy:

<MathBlock id="1.104" name="differential entropy">

$$H[x] = -\int p(x)\ln p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$$

</MathBlock>

Maximised, for a fixed mean and variance, by the Gaussian:

<MathBlock id="1.110" name="differential entropy of a Gaussian">

$$H[x] = \frac{1}{2}\big(1 + \ln(2\pi\sigma^2)\big)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Unlike discrete entropy, this can go negative: concentrating a density enough costs it below the zero a discrete count could never cross." source="1.110">
  <DifferentialEntropyNegative client:visible />
</Figure>

Coding for an approximation $q$ of a true $p$ costs extra, on average:

<MathBlock id="1.113" name="Kullback-Leibler divergence">

$$\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = -\int p(x)\ln\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x$$

</MathBlock>

Applied to a joint distribution against the product of its own marginals, this becomes
mutual information, zero exactly at independence:

<MathBlock id="1.120" name="mutual information">

$$I[x,y] = \mathrm{KL}\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\big)$$

</MathBlock>

which splits a joint entropy additively:

<MathBlock id="1.112" name="conditional entropy identity">

$$H[x,y] = H[x] + H[y \mid x]$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

$\mathrm{KL}$ is not symmetric. Fixing $p=\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $q=\mathcal{N}(2,2.25)$:
$\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$ and $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)$ come out to two different numbers from the same
pair of curves, because the cost of the wrong code depends on which distribution is doing
the actual generating.

PRML's own Exercise 1.39: $p(x{=}0,y{=}0){=}p(x{=}0,y{=}1){=}p(x{=}1,y{=}1){=}1/3$,
$p(x{=}1,y{=}0){=}0$. Computing all six quantities from that one table: $H[x]=H[y]=0.6365$,
$H[x,y]=1.0986$, $H[y|x]=H[x|y]=0.4621$, $I[x,y]=0.1744$ nats, small but nonzero, since
$x=1$ rules out $y=0$ outright.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why must information content be a logarithm of probability?" concepts={["information-content"]}>
Requiring the information from two independent events to add, h(x,y)=h(x)+h(y), while
their probabilities multiply, p(x,y)=p(x)p(y), pins the functional form to a logarithm.
</Recall>

<Recall question="In what precise sense does entropy measure coding difficulty?" concepts={["entropy"]}>
It is the shortest average code length achievable for outcomes drawn from that
distribution: PRML's own 8-state example reaches exactly its entropy, 2 bits, with a
length-matched prefix code, and no code can do better on average.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can differential entropy be negative when discrete entropy cannot?" concepts={["differential-entropy"]}>
Differential entropy measures relative to a continuous coordinate scale rather than
counting discrete states, so concentrating a density tightly enough drives it below zero,
a case with no discrete analogue.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is KL divergence not symmetric, and what does that mean practically?" concepts={["kl-divergence"]}>
It weights the log-ratio by p, the true distribution, so which distribution is "true" in
the pair changes the answer: approximating a broad p with a narrow q costs differently
than the reverse.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does it mean for mutual information to equal zero?" concepts={["mutual-information"]}>
That the two variables are independent: their joint distribution already equals the
product of its marginals, so there is no divergence left for KL to measure.
</Recall>
