## Intuition

Take some noisy points and draw a curve through them. That is the whole problem, and
everything else in this book is an answer to "which curve, and why."

A curve with too few bends cannot follow the shape the data came from. A curve with too
many bends follows the data too well, tracing individual points rather than the pattern
behind them. Both fail at the same task, prediction on a point you have not seen, and the
failures look nothing alike: one is visibly too simple, the other fits so closely it looks
like success until you check it against new data.

The unsettling part is that nothing in the fitting procedure itself flags the second
failure. Asked to make the training data more probable, an overly flexible curve obliges,
and the error you were minimising quietly stops meaning what you thought it meant. Finding
a way to notice this, without a warning built into the number you were watching, motivates
most of the rest of the chapter.

## The math

A degree-$M$ polynomial, linear in its coefficients however curved in $x$:

<MathBlock id="1.1" name="polynomial function">

$$y(x, \mathbf{w}) = w_0 + w_1 x + w_2 x^2 + \dots + w_M x^M = \sum_{j=0}^{M} w_j x^j$$

</MathBlock>

Fit it by minimising the sum of squared errors against $N$ training points:

<MathBlock id="1.2" name="sum-of-squares error">

$$E(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\{y(x_n, \mathbf{w}) - t_n\}^2$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Each dashed segment is one term of 1.2 before it is squared." source="1.3">
  <ErrorFunctionGeometry client:visible />
</Figure>

$E$ is quadratic in $\mathbf{w}$, so its minimum is a linear system with a unique closed-form
solution. Comparing fits of different sizes fairly needs a scale-free number:

<MathBlock id="1.3" name="root-mean-square error">

$$E_{\mathrm{RMS}} = \sqrt{2E(\mathbf{w}^\star)/N}$$

</MathBlock>

Dividing by $N$ makes datasets of different sizes comparable; the square root returns the
number to the units $t$ is measured in. A penalty on large coefficients keeps the closed
form and buys a second knob besides $M$:

<MathBlock id="1.4" name="regularised error function">

$$\tilde{E}(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\{y(x_n, \mathbf{w}) - t_n\}^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

Ten points, $t_n = \sin(2\pi x_n)$ plus noise, fit at $M = 0,1,3,9$: RMS goes
$0.65 \to 0.60 \to 0.19 \to 0.00$ on the training set but $0.73 \to 0.58 \to 0.26 \to 0.74$
on a held-out set of 100, so $M=9$ wins on the number it was optimised against and loses
everywhere else. Its largest coefficient reaches $4.6\times10^5$, against $3.6\times10^1$
at $M=3$.

<Figure caption="Coefficient size by order, log scale. Growth by three or four decades marks where the fit starts spending its freedom on noise rather than shape." width="wide">
  <CoefficientMagnitudes client:visible />
</Figure>

Two fixes, same $M=9$ model. More data: at $N=100$ the identical procedure gets test RMS
down to $0.23$, because ten points is not enough to outvote nine coefficients' worth of
noise-fitting.

<Figure caption="Same M, same generating function, four times the data. The oscillation between points is not gone, only pushed down." width="wide">
  <OverfittingWithN client:visible />
</Figure>

Or a penalty: $\ln\lambda = -18$ brings test RMS to $0.31$ without adding a single point,
while $\ln\lambda = 0$ over-corrects and pushes it back up to $0.60$.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the training error alone fail to catch over-fitting?" concepts={["overfitting"]}>
Training error is exactly what the fit minimised, so an increasingly flexible model can
always lower it further by reproducing noise. Only performance on data the fit never saw
reveals the difference.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the lambda term in 1.4 add over choosing M alone?" concepts={["regularisation"]}>
A second, continuous knob on effective complexity. Rather than discrete jumps between
polynomial orders, lambda shrinks coefficients smoothly, trading training fit for
simplicity without changing M.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why divide by N inside the RMS error?" concepts={["rms-error"]}>
So that a training set of 10 points and one of 100 can be compared on the same footing;
without it, sum-of-squares error naturally grows with N regardless of fit quality.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Two different M=9 fits both reach zero training error on ten points. What can differ between them?" concepts={["overfitting", "polynomial-curve-fitting"]}>
Everything between the data points. Ten constraints pin down ten coefficients exactly, so
any noise realisation produces a different, wildly oscillating curve even though every one
of them fits the training data perfectly.
</Recall>
