## Intuition

Probability theory says how uncertain you should be. It never says what to do about it.
Told a patient has a 70% chance of a condition, the model has done its job; whether to
treat is a separate question, and probability theory has nothing more to add to it.

That second question turns out to have a close-to-mechanical answer once the first is
settled: pick whichever action has the lowest expected cost, given everything currently
believed. The subtlety is not in the mechanism, it is in what "cost" means. Treating a
healthy patient and missing a sick one are both mistakes, but rarely equally bad ones, and
a decision rule that cannot tell them apart cannot be tuned to care more about the worse
one.

This section separates the two questions cleanly: work out the uncertainty, then decide.
Collapsing them into a single learned function is possible and sometimes cheaper, but it
throws away everything the extra structure was for.

## The math

For two classes, the probability of a mistake splits by which region a point falls in:

<MathBlock id="1.78" name="probability of a mistake">

$$p(\text{mistake}) = \int_{\mathcal{R}_1}p(x,C_2)\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_{\mathcal{R}_2}p(x,C_1)\,\mathrm{d}x$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Drag the boundary. The shaded area is exactly 1.78, and it is smallest where the two curves cross." source="1.24">
  <DecisionBoundaryExplorer client:visible />
</Figure>

Minimised by assigning each $x$ to the largest $p(C_k\mid x)$. A loss matrix $L_{kj}$
generalises this to unequal costs by choosing, for each $x$, whichever class minimises:

<MathBlock id="1.81" name="expected-loss decision rule">

$$\sum_k L_{kj}\,p(C_k \mid x)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Sliding the loss ratio moves the boundary away from equal, toward whichever class is expensive to miss." source="1.25">
  <LossMatrixShift client:visible />
</Figure>

A reject option refuses to decide wherever the largest posterior falls under a threshold
$\theta$:

<Figure caption="Raise theta and the shaded reject band widens from nothing to everything.">
  <RejectRegion client:visible />
</Figure>

Generative models fit $p(x\mid C_k)$ for each class and combine with Bayes' theorem;
discriminative models fit $p(C_k\mid x)$ directly. The posterior can be far simpler than
the densities that produced it:

<Figure caption="A two-humped class density collapses to a single smooth posterior step; the second hump barely moves it." source="1.27">
  <ClassDensityVsPosterior client:visible />
</Figure>

For regression, expected loss under a general loss function is:

<MathBlock id="1.86" name="expected loss, general">

$$\mathbb{E}[L] = \iint L(t, y(x))\,p(x, t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t$$

</MathBlock>

Under squared loss this is minimised by the conditional mean:

<MathBlock id="1.89" name="regression function">

$$y(x) = \int t\,p(t\mid x)\,\mathrm{d}t = \mathbb{E}_t[t \mid x]$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Left: a two-humped p(t|x). Right: expected squared loss against a candidate y, minimised exactly at the conditional mean even though that value itself is unlikely.">
  <ExpectedLossMinimizer client:visible />
</Figure>

Squared loss is one choice among a family:

<MathBlock id="1.91" name="Minkowski loss">

$$\mathbb{E}[L_q] = \iint |y(x) - t|^q\,p(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

$q=2$ minimises at the mean, $q=1$ at the median. On targets $\{1,2,2,3,3,10\}$ the mean is
$3.50$ but the median is $2.50$: one outlier at $10$ drags the mean well above every other
point while the median, which only cares about order, ignores it completely.

<Figure caption="The four loss shapes behind that difference; q=2 punishes the outlier quadratically, q=1 linearly." width="wide">
  <MinkowskiLossShapes client:visible />
</Figure>

Whichever loss is appropriate depends on whether a single bad measurement should dominate
the fit or be shrugged off, a modelling choice the squared-loss default hides.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does minimising p(mistake) mean choosing the largest posterior p(Ck|x)?" concepts={["decision-boundary"]}>
p(mistake) integrates the losing class's joint density over each region; making that
integrand as small as possible everywhere means picking whichever class has the larger
p(x,Ck), which is the same as the larger posterior once p(x) cancels.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What can a loss matrix express that plain misclassification rate cannot?" concepts={["loss-matrix", "expected-loss"]}>
That different kinds of mistakes cost different amounts. Minimising expected loss instead
of error rate shifts the boundary toward the cheaper kind of mistake, away from equal.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why would you want posterior probabilities rather than only a discriminant function?" concepts={["discriminant-function", "generative-model"]}>
Posteriors let a loss matrix change after training, support a reject option, and combine
with other models, all without retraining. A discriminant function collapses straight to a
label and keeps none of that.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the second mode of a class-conditional density in the worked figure teach about generative modelling?" concepts={["generative-model", "discriminative-model"]}>
That fitting p(x|Ck) precisely can spend effort on structure with almost no effect on the
posterior, and hence none on the decision. A discriminative model never pays that cost.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is the conditional mean the optimal prediction under squared loss even when it is an unlikely value of t?" concepts={["regression-function"]}>
Squared loss punishes distance quadratically regardless of density, so the minimiser
balances distances rather than tracking where probability mass sits; a bimodal p(t|x) can
have its mean sit in a valley between the modes.
</Recall>
