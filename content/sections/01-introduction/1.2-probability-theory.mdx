## Intuition

Uncertainty shows up twice in the curve-fitting example: noise on the targets, and not
knowing which weights are right. Both need the same calculus, and it turns out to rest on
exactly two rules, one for combining probabilities and one for reversing which variable
is conditioned on which.

That second rule lets you flip a probability you can state, how likely this data is under
a guess, into the one you actually want, how likely the guess is given the data. Frequentist
statistics stops at the first kind. The Bayesian view treats the guess itself, not just the
data, as uncertain, and updates that uncertainty rather than committing to one value.

Applied to curve fitting, this replaces a single best-fit polynomial with a whole weighted
family of them, and it is what finally explains, rather than merely penalises, over-fitting.

## The math

Two rules generate everything else:

<MathBlock id="1.10" name="sum rule">

$$p(X) = \sum_Y p(X, Y)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="1.11" name="product rule">

$$p(X, Y) = p(Y \mid X)\,p(X)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="A joint table over 9 x-states and 2 y-states. Summing a column gives the marginal; restricting to one row and renormalising gives a conditional." source="1.11">
  <JointMarginalConditional client:visible />
</Figure>

Symmetry of the joint, $p(X,Y)=p(Y,X)$, turns the product rule into Bayes' theorem:

<MathBlock id="1.12" name="Bayes' theorem">

$$p(Y \mid X) = \frac{p(X \mid Y)\,p(Y)}{p(X)}$$

</MathBlock>

The Gaussian, used for noise throughout the chapter:

<MathBlock id="1.46" name="Gaussian distribution">

$$\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Drag both parameters. The peak height and the ±1σ marks move together; the area under the curve never changes." source="1.13">
  <GaussianDraggable client:visible />
</Figure>

Its maximum-likelihood fit to $N$ points is the sample mean and sample variance:

<MathBlock id="1.55" name="maximum likelihood mean">

$$\mu_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} x_n$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="1.56" name="maximum likelihood variance">

$$\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(x_n - \mu_{\mathrm{ML}})^2$$

</MathBlock>

but the variance estimate is biased low:

<MathBlock id="1.58" name="expected maximum likelihood variance">

$$\mathbb{E}[\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}] = \frac{N-1}{N}\sigma^2$$

</MathBlock>

Put a Gaussian prior on the curve-fitting weights and maximising the posterior is
minimising a familiar, penalised error:

<MathBlock id="1.65" name="Gaussian prior over weights">

$$p(\mathbf{w} \mid \alpha) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{0}, \alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I})$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="1.67" name="MAP objective">

$$\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\{y(x_n,\mathbf{w}) - t_n\}^2 + \frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{w}$$

</MathBlock>

which is 1.4 with $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$: regularisation is a prior in disguise. Going
fully Bayesian means never collapsing $\mathbf{w}$ to one value, but integrating over all
of it:

<MathBlock id="1.68" name="predictive distribution">

$$p(t \mid x, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t}) = \int p(t \mid x, \mathbf{w})\,p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

Box A (2 apples, 6 oranges, prior 0.4), Box B (3 apples, 1 orange, prior 0.6). Overall
$p(\text{apple}) = 0.55$. Told the fruit was an orange, the posterior on Box A rises to
$2/3$: an orange is rarer from Box B, so observing one favours Box A even though Box B was
the likelier box beforehand.

<Figure caption="Switch the observed fruit and watch both posteriors move. Only the likelihoods differ; the prior is fixed." source="1.9">
  <TwoBoxMarbles client:visible />
</Figure>

Fitting $\mu_{\mathrm{ML}}$ and $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}$ to $20{,}000$ simulated samples of
size $N=2$ from a unit-variance Gaussian gives a mean estimate of $0.497$, matching 1.58's
prediction of $0.5$ almost exactly; by $N=50$ the gap has shrunk to under $0.02$.

<Figure caption="Simulated, not asserted: 20000 fits per N, plotted against 1.58's prediction." width="wide">
  <MLVarianceBias client:visible />
</Figure>

The same asymmetry shows up in curve fitting: the fitted posterior's predictive std stays
near $0.23$ inside the training range but reaches $23$ at $x=1.3$, just past its edge.

## Recall

<Recall question="What does the product rule let you do that the sum rule alone cannot?" concepts={["product-rule", "bayes-theorem"]}>
Reverse a conditional. Combined with the symmetry of a joint distribution it gives Bayes'
theorem, which turns p(data | guess) into p(guess | data).
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is the maximum likelihood variance estimate biased?" concepts={["estimator-bias"]}>
It measures spread around the sample mean, which itself was fit to minimise that spread,
rather than around the true mean. That double duty is what 1.58's factor (N-1)/N corrects.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What turns regularised least squares into a Bayesian statement?" concepts={["maximum-a-posteriori", "hyperparameter"]}>
Putting a Gaussian prior of precision alpha on the weights. Maximising the resulting
posterior is exactly 1.4 with lambda = alpha/beta: a penalty term is a prior in disguise.
</Recall>

<Recall question="How does the Bayesian predictive distribution differ from the maximum likelihood one?" concepts={["predictive-distribution"]}>
It integrates over every weight setting instead of plugging in one. The result still has a
Gaussian form, but its variance grows with distance from the data, not only from the fixed
noise term.
</Recall>

<Recall question="In the boxes example, why does observing an orange favour Box A despite Box B being chosen more often?" concepts={["bayes-theorem"]}>
The likelihood ratio dominates the prior ratio: oranges are three times commoner in Box A
than Box B, more than enough to outweigh Box A being picked less often to begin with.
</Recall>
