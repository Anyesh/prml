## Intuition

Every proposal so far has ignored the one piece of information a smooth target hands over
for free: which direction is uphill. A random walk wanders blindly and pays for it with
steps that grow only as the square root of how long it has run. Hybrid Monte Carlo instead
treats the negative log target as a landscape and lets a simulated ball roll across it,
using the gradient to keep moving in a consistent direction for many steps in a row before
ever checking whether to accept the result.

Rolling a ball needs a speed as well as a position, so the state gains a second half:
momentum, invented purely to make the physics work and then never reported to the reader.
The two decisions gradient descent glosses over, when to reject a bad move and when to
inject fresh randomness, are exactly what turn this deterministic-feeling rollout back into
a valid sampler.

## The math

The state gains momentum $r$, with kinetic energy

<MathBlock id="11.56" name="kinetic energy">

$$K(r) = \frac{1}{2}\lVert r \rVert^2$$

</MathBlock>

added to the potential $E(z) = -\ln \tilde{p}(z)$ to form the conserved Hamiltonian

<MathBlock id="11.57" name="the Hamiltonian">

$$H(z, r) = E(z) + K(r)$$

</MathBlock>

The leapfrog integrator advances this in discrete time by alternating half-step momentum
updates with full-step position updates:

<MathBlock id="11.64" name="leapfrog momentum half-step">

$$r_i(\tau + \epsilon/2) = r_i(\tau) - \frac{\epsilon}{2}\frac{\partial E}{\partial z_i}(z(\tau))$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="11.65" name="leapfrog position full-step">

$$z_i(\tau + \epsilon) = z_i(\tau) + \epsilon\, r_i(\tau + \epsilon/2)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="11.66" name="leapfrog momentum second half-step">

$$r_i(\tau + \epsilon) = r_i(\tau + \epsilon/2) - \frac{\epsilon}{2}\frac{\partial E}{\partial z_i}(z(\tau + \epsilon))$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="A square patch of phase space becomes a parallelogram under one leapfrog step, with its area unchanged." width="wide">
  <LeapfrogShearing client:visible />
</Figure>

This shearing is exactly why the scheme preserves phase-space volume even though it only
approximates the true trajectory; the residual error is what the accept step then removes:

<MathBlock id="11.67" name="hybrid Monte Carlo acceptance probability">

$$\min\big(1, \exp\{H(z, r) - H(z^\star, r^\star)\}\big)$$

</MathBlock>

On a 2-D Gaussian with $\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ set by `cov = [[2, 0], [0, 0.05]]`
(ratio $\approx 6.3$), a leapfrog step of $\epsilon = 0.05$ keeps $H$ within about 0.02 of
its starting value over 80 steps; $\epsilon = 0.35$ lets it swing by nearly a full unit, the
error (11.67)'s Metropolis criterion exists to catch.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does hybrid Monte Carlo introduce a momentum variable that plays no role in the final answer?" concepts={["hamiltonian-dynamics"]}>
Momentum is what lets the Hamiltonian framework's conserved-energy and volume-preservation
properties apply at all; only the position half of the resulting trajectory is kept.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does the leapfrog integrator preserve phase-space volume even though it does not exactly solve the dynamics?" concepts={["leapfrog-integration"]}>
Each step changes one variable by an amount depending only on the other, which shears
regions of phase space without changing their area or volume (Figure 11.14).
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the accept/reject step in hybrid Monte Carlo actually correct for?" concepts={["hybrid-monte-carlo"]}>
The residual numerical error the leapfrog integrator introduces for a nonzero step size,
measured directly as however much H has drifted from its starting value (11.67).
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does hybrid Monte Carlo need the step size or its sign randomised between trajectories?" concepts={["hybrid-monte-carlo"]}>
A fixed step size and direction can make the leapfrog map periodic, returning to its start
and leaving position updates stuck; randomising breaks that periodicity.
</Recall>
