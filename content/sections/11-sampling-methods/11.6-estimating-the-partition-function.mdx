## Intuition

Every method in this chapter has quietly leaned on the same convenience: none of them ever
needed to know how a distribution's unnormalized shape scales to integrate to one. That
missing constant, the partition function, was never in the way. Now it is the answer.

Bayesian model comparison needs the ratio of two models' evidences, and that ratio is
exactly a ratio of partition functions. Estimating it sounds like it should reuse
importance sampling directly, and it does, but only when the two distributions being
compared are already close. Stretch that comparison across two very different
distributions and the estimate becomes exactly as unreliable as importance sampling always
is under mismatch: technically unbiased, practically useless.

## The math

Any distribution built from an energy function carries an unknown normalizer:

<MathBlock id="11.71" name="energy-based unnormalized distribution">

$$p_E(z) = \frac{1}{Z_E}\exp(-E(z))$$

</MathBlock>

Importance sampling from a tractable $p_G$ estimates the ratio of two such normalizers
directly:

<MathBlock id="11.72" name="partition-function ratio by importance sampling">

$$\frac{Z_E}{Z_G} \simeq \sum_l \exp\{-E(z^{(l)}) + G(z^{(l)})\}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="A ladder of distributions between a tractable proposal and the target, one interpolated energy at a time." width="wide">
  <ChainingIntermediateDistributions client:visible />
</Figure>

Chaining turns one large, unreliable ratio into a product of small, reliable ones:

<MathBlock id="11.74" name="chaining a sequence of ratios">

$$\frac{Z_M}{Z_1} = \frac{Z_2}{Z_1}\frac{Z_3}{Z_2}\cdots\frac{Z_M}{Z_{M-1}}$$

</MathBlock>

built from a family of energies interpolating linearly between the two endpoints:

<MathBlock id="11.75" name="linearly interpolated energy">

$$E_\alpha(z) = (1-\alpha)E_1(z) + \alpha E_M(z)$$

</MathBlock>

Neither method is biased in principle; the practical difference is entirely in the spread.

## Worked example

<Figure caption="Direct importance sampling's spread grows fastest as the proposal drifts; chaining through six links grows far more slowly." width="wide">
  <SpreadVsMismatch client:visible />
</Figure>

With target scale 1 and proposal scale 2.5, 16 repeats of the direct ratio estimate span
0.363 to 0.457 around a true value of 0.4, a width of 0.094. Chaining the same comparison
through six intermediate distributions, with the same total number of samples, narrows that
to a width of 0.061, because no individual link is ever asked to bridge the whole gap alone.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why did none of the earlier sections in this chapter need the partition function?" concepts={["partition-function-estimation"]}>
Every sampler only compares p(z) at different points of z, and any overall scaling
constant cancels out of that comparison; it never entered the acceptance rule or the
weight formula.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the ratio of two partition functions give you in a Bayesian setting?" concepts={["partition-function-estimation"]}>
Multiplied by the ratio of prior probabilities, it gives the ratio of posterior model
probabilities: it is literally a ratio of model evidences, computed a different way.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does chaining help even though the direct estimate is already unbiased?" concepts={["partition-function-estimation"]}>
Unbiasedness says nothing about spread. Each link in the chain compares two nearly
identical distributions, so its own estimate is low-variance; multiplying several
low-variance ratios together stays far tighter than one high-variance ratio spanning the
same total gap.
</Recall>
