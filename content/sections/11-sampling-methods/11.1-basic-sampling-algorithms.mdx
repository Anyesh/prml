## Intuition

Every method here answers the same question: given a machine that spits out uniform random
numbers, how do you get samples from a distribution nobody handed you a generator for. The
honest answer is that none of the tricks in this section scale. Each one turns randomness
into more randomness in a way that works cleanly on a blackboard and quietly falls apart as
the number of variables grows.

Rejection sampling wraps the target in an envelope and throws away everything that lands
outside it. Importance sampling never throws anything away, but instead pays for that by
letting a handful of lucky points carry the whole answer. Both failures are really the same
failure: matching a simple proposal to a complicated target gets exponentially harder as
dimension grows, and neither method notices when it has failed.

## The math

Sampling from a uniform source and wanting some other density $p(y)$ means inverting its
own cumulative integral:

<MathBlock id="11.6" name="inverse transform method">

$$z = h(y) \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{y} p(y')\,\mathrm{d}y'$$

</MathBlock>

The exact algorithm behind `standardNormal` in this codebase is this method's most useful
instance: pairs uniform on $(-1,1)$ are kept only inside the unit disk, then mapped through
a closed-form inverse to a Gaussian pair.

<Figure caption="One seed's attempts: crosses fall outside the disk and get redrawn; the surviving circle becomes a Gaussian pair." source="11.3">
  <BoxMullerCircle client:visible />
</Figure>

Most targets have no such inverse. Rejection sampling instead only needs $p(z)$ up to a
constant,

<MathBlock id="11.13" name="unnormalized target density">

$$p(z) = \frac{1}{Z_p}\tilde{p}(z)$$

</MathBlock>

and accepts a proposal draw with probability $\tilde{p}(z)/k q(z)$, so the overall accept
rate is exactly

<MathBlock id="11.14" name="rejection sampling acceptance probability">

$$p(\text{accept}) = \frac{1}{k}\int \tilde{p}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z$$

</MathBlock>

Adaptive rejection sampling builds $k q(z)$ from tangent lines to $\ln p(z)$, giving a
piecewise exponential envelope,

<MathBlock id="11.17" name="piecewise-exponential envelope">

$$q(z) = k_i \lambda_i \exp\{-\lambda_i (z - z_{i-1})\}, \quad z_{i-1} < z \leqslant z_i$$

</MathBlock>

that only tightens on every rejection. Importance sampling drops the accept/reject step
entirely and instead reweights every draw by

<MathBlock id="11.23" name="normalized importance weights">

$$w_l = \frac{p(z^{(l)})/q(z^{(l)})}{\sum_m p(z^{(m)})/q(z^{(m)})}$$

</MathBlock>

Finally, when an E-step integral has no closed form, the same finite-sum idea approximates
it directly:

<MathBlock id="11.28" name="Monte Carlo EM's expected complete-data log likelihood">

$$Q(\theta,\theta^{\text{old}}) \simeq \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L} \ln p(Z^{(l)}, X \mid \theta)$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

<Figure caption="Six accepted draws later, every rejection has added a tangent line and the envelope has visibly tightened." width="wide">
  <AdaptiveRejectionEnvelope client:visible />
</Figure>

Starting from grid points $\{-2, -0.5, 0, 1, 3\}$ on a standard normal, the first envelope
already bounds the target everywhere; each rejected candidate folds in as a sixth, seventh,
eighth tangent line, so the accept rate only ever rises.

<Figure caption="Twenty-four proposal draws (top) reweighted, then twenty-four fresh draws taken from those weights (bottom)." width="wide">
  <SirResampling client:visible />
</Figure>

Sampling-importance-resampling turns those same importance weights into an actual sample
set: draw from Exp(1), weight by $\text{Gam}(z\mid 3,1)/\text{Exp}(1)$, then resample. Points
the proposal rarely favoured are almost never chosen twice.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does a Monte Carlo estimator's accuracy not depend on the dimension of z?" concepts={["monte-carlo-estimator"]}>
Its variance is $\mathrm{var}[f]/L$ (11.3), a property of $f$ under $p$ and the sample count,
not of how many coordinates $z$ has.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What single number does rejection sampling's acceptance rate depend on?" concepts={["rejection-sampling"]}>
$1/k$: the ratio of area under the target to area under the envelope (11.14). Everything
about the method's cost is that one constant.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is importance sampling more dangerous than rejection sampling when it fails?" concepts={["importance-sampling", "effective-sample-size"]}>
Rejection sampling fails visibly, as a low acceptance rate. Importance sampling can report
a confident, badly wrong number with no warning; effective sample size is the diagnostic
that catches it after the fact.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does adaptive rejection sampling's envelope only ever improve?" concepts={["adaptive-rejection-sampling"]}>
Every rejected point becomes a new tangent line, so the log-concave envelope can only get
tighter, never looser, as sampling proceeds.
</Recall>

<Recall question="In sampling-importance-resampling, when are the resampled points exactly distributed as p(z)?" concepts={["sampling-importance-resampling"]}>
Only in the limit of infinitely many proposal draws (11.26); for finite L the result is an
approximation that improves as q gets closer to p.
</Recall>
