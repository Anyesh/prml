## Intuition

Ridge regression from chapter 3 solves for a handful of weights, one per basis function.
There is a second way to solve the exact same problem: instead of one coefficient per
feature, keep one coefficient per training point, and write the prediction as a weighted
sum over the data itself. Nothing about the fit changes; the same curve comes out.

What changes is what the solution touches. In this second form, every training point enters
only through a comparison with another point, never as a raw feature vector. That single
shift is the seed of the whole chapter: once an algorithm only needs comparisons between
inputs, those comparisons can be defined directly, in a feature space nobody ever builds.

## The math

A kernel is a symmetric comparison standing in for an inner product in some feature space:

<MathBlock id="6.1" name="kernel function">

$$k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}')$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Four points, one RBF kernel, sixteen numbers: every entry is k(xₙ, xₘ) for a pair of training points." source="6.1">
  <GramMatrixSmallExample client:visible />
</Figure>

Setting the gradient of the ridge objective $J(\mathbf{w})$ to zero shows $\mathbf{w}$ is
already a combination of the data:

<MathBlock id="6.3" name="weights as a combination of the data">

$$\mathbf{w} = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n) = \boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{a}$$

</MathBlock>

Substituting this back and collecting every $\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_m)$
into the Gram matrix,

<MathBlock id="6.6" name="Gram matrix">

$$K_{nm} = \boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_m) = k(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x}_m)$$

</MathBlock>

gives a solution and a prediction in kernel evaluations alone:

<MathBlock id="6.8" name="dual solution">

$$\mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{K} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_N)^{-1}\mathbf{t}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="6.9" name="dual prediction">

$$y(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{K} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_N)^{-1}\mathbf{t}$$

</MathBlock>

6.8 inverts $N \times N$; the weight-space route inverts $M \times M$. With $M \ll N$,
weight space is cheaper, which is why this looks like a bad trade until $\boldsymbol{\phi}$
stops being something you can afford to write down.

</Figure>

## Worked example

Six points, a degree-2 polynomial basis ($M = 3$), $\lambda = 0.05$. Weight space gives
$\mathbf{w} \approx (-0.013, -0.811, 1.740)$; dual space gives six coefficients $a_n$, the
largest $9.39$ in magnitude, the rest a mix of positive and negative.

<Figure caption="Six coefficients, one per training point, computed from the same fit the curve above plots." source="6.1">
  <DualVsPrimalWorkedNumbers client:visible />
</Figure>

At $x = 0.3$, both routes give $y \approx -0.1000$; at $x = 0.85$, both give
$y \approx 0.5544$. They agree to numerical precision because 6.8 is not an approximation
to the weight-space solution, it is the same stationary point of the same $J(\mathbf{w})$,
reached from the other side.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the dual formulation invert an N by N matrix instead of an M by M one?" concepts={["dual-representation"]}>
Because 6.8 solves for one coefficient per training point, $\mathbf{a}$, rather than one
per basis function. The size of the matrix follows whichever quantity you solve for.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Does the dual solution ever predict a different curve from the weight-space one?" concepts={["dual-representation"]}>
No. Both are the same stationary point of the same regularised error, reached by
substituting 6.3 into $J(\mathbf{w})$ rather than by any approximation.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the Gram matrix actually collect?" concepts={["gram-matrix"]}>
Every pairwise kernel evaluation between training points, $K_{nm} = k(\mathbf{x}_n,
\mathbf{x}_m)$: a symmetric $N \times N$ table built once and reused by 6.8 and 6.9.
</Recall>

<Recall question="When is the dual formulation actually cheaper to compute?" concepts={["dual-representation"]}>
Only once the feature count $M$ exceeds the data count $N$. Below that crossing, weight
space wins on cost alone; the real payoff is not needing to construct $\boldsymbol{\phi}$.
</Recall>
