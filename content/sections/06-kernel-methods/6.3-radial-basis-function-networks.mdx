## Intuition

3.6 raised the coverage problem as fatal for a fixed basis: tiling space before seeing the
data costs a count that explodes with dimension, and the tiles cannot move once placed.

A radial basis function sidesteps the placement question entirely by not choosing centres
ahead of time at all. Put one bump on every training point instead. Coverage stops being a
guess, because the basis literally sits wherever the data landed.

This costs something too. A basis with one function per point grows with the dataset rather
than staying fixed, and evaluating it gets slower as more data arrives. What looked like the
fatal flaw of a fixed basis becomes, from this side, the price of not needing to guess.

## The math

Centring a basis function on every point and solving by least squares interpolates the
data exactly:

<MathBlock id="6.38" name="exact radial basis interpolation">

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n h(\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_n\|)$$

</MathBlock>

Treating the *inputs* as noisy rather than the targets, and optimising by the calculus of
variations, gives the same shape of model with a different, self-normalising basis:

<MathBlock id="6.41" name="basis functions from noisy inputs">

$$h(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_n) = \frac{\nu(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_n)}{\sum_{m} \nu(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_m)}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Raw Gaussian bumps fade outside their centres; dividing each by the row total keeps every point covered." source="6.2" width="wide">
  <NormalisedBasisFunctions client:visible />
</Figure>

Starting instead from a Parzen estimate of the joint density of inputs and targets and
taking the conditional mean gives the identical form from a second, independent route:

<MathBlock id="6.45" name="Nadaraya-Watson prediction">

$$y(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{n} k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_n)t_n$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="6.46" name="Nadaraya-Watson weights">

$$k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_n) = \frac{g(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_n)}{\sum_{m} g(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_m)}$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

Fourteen points from the same sinusoidal set-up as chapter 3, one narrow bandwidth and one
wide one.

<Figure caption="A bandwidth an order of magnitude apart either way, against the true curve." source="6.3">
  <BandwidthUnderOverSmoothing client:visible />
</Figure>

Against the true sine, $h = 0.015$ scores a mean squared error of $0.071$ and $h = 0.5$
scores $0.342$: both worse than the $0.053$ a bandwidth of $0.08$ reaches on this same
dataset, but oversmoothing here costs far more than undersmoothing does.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does 3.6's coverage argument stop being fatal for a radial basis?" concepts={["radial-basis-function"]}>
Because the centres are the training points themselves, chosen after seeing the data
rather than laid out in advance. Coverage follows automatically; cost per prediction rises.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What guarantees the Nadaraya-Watson weights sum to one at every x?" concepts={["nadaraya-watson-model"]}>
Dividing by the sum in the denominator of (6.46) directly, so whatever the numerator's
shape, the weights it produces always add to exactly one.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What derivation gives the Nadaraya-Watson model besides noisy inputs?" concepts={["nadaraya-watson-model"]}>
A Parzen density estimate of the joint distribution of inputs and targets, with the
conditional mean read off directly. Two routes, one formula.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What happens to the prediction at the extremes of the bandwidth?" concepts={["nadaraya-watson-model"]}>
Too small, and the curve interpolates whichever point is nearest, chasing noise between
points; too large, and every point contributes almost equally, flattening the curve.
</Recall>
