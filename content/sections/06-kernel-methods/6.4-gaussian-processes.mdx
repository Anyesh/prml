## Intuition

3.3 put a prior over weights and asked what that implies about predictions. Read the same
setup the other way: the prior over weights already induces a distribution over the function
values themselves, and that distribution is Gaussian regardless of what the weights mean.

Once that is the object of interest, the weights can be dropped entirely. Describe the
function values directly, as a joint Gaussian whose covariance is a kernel, and every result
chapter 3 derived by way of a prior over weights reappears, without ever naming a weight.

The payoff is not smaller equations. It is that a covariance function can encode structure no
finite basis could, and fitting it means adjusting a handful of interpretable knobs rather
than choosing a basis before seeing a single point.

## The math

A weight-space model already fixes the distribution over function values at any inputs:

<MathBlock id="6.49" name="linear model in the weights">

$$y(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="6.54" name="covariance induced by the weight prior">

$$K_{nm} = k(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x}_m) = \frac{1}{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_m)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Weights drawn from the prior and evaluated, against functions drawn straight from the kernel that same prior implies: the same distribution, generated two ways." width="wide">
  <WeightSpaceEquivalence client:visible />
</Figure>

A Gaussian process makes this primary: any kernel, not only one built from a finite
$\boldsymbol{\phi}$, specifies a valid distribution over functions directly.

<Figure caption="theta1 sets how fast sampled functions wiggle; theta0 sets how tall they get." width="wide">
  <KernelHyperparameterEffects client:visible />
</Figure>

<Figure caption="Correlation between two points fades with distance at a rate theta1 controls; every sampled function stays smooth over exactly that width." width="wide">
  <CovarianceHeatmapAndSamples client:visible />
</Figure>

Observation noise adds directly to the covariance, and a composite kernel gives four
independent knobs to fit:

<MathBlock id="6.62" name="covariance with observation noise">

$$C(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x}_m) = k(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x}_m) + \beta^{-1}\delta_{nm}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="6.63" name="composite covariance function">

$$k(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x}_m) = \theta_0\exp\!\left(-\frac{\theta_1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{x}_m\|^2\right) + \theta_2 + \theta_3\mathbf{x}_n^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}_m$$

</MathBlock>

Two points closer than the kernel's own length scale make this near-singular; Cholesky
needs a jittered diagonal to factor it at all.

<Figure caption="A tenth point sliding toward an existing one: condition number climbs, and unjittered Cholesky eventually throws." width="wide">
  <JitterNecessity client:visible />
</Figure>

Conditioning the joint Gaussian on training targets gives the predictive distribution:

<MathBlock id="6.66" name="GP predictive mean">

$$m(\mathbf{x}_{N+1}) = \mathbf{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{t}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="6.67" name="GP predictive variance">

$$\sigma^2(\mathbf{x}_{N+1}) = c - \mathbf{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{k}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Variance sits near the noise floor between training points and climbs toward the prior variance past either edge." width="wide">
  <PredictiveVarianceGrowsAway client:visible />
</Figure>

Fitting $\theta_0 \ldots \theta_3$ maximises the marginal likelihood directly, using its
gradient:

<MathBlock id="6.69" name="log marginal likelihood">

$$\ln p(\mathbf{t}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln|\mathbf{C}_N| - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{t} - \tfrac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="6.70" name="log marginal likelihood gradient">

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_i}\ln p(\mathbf{t}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{C}_N}{\partial\theta_i}\right) + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{C}_N}{\partial\theta_i}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{t}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The surface 6.70's gradient climbs, grid-searched here, optimum marked." width="wide">
  <LogMarginalLikelihoodSurface client:visible />
</Figure>

Giving every input dimension its own length scale and fitting them the same way is automatic
relevance determination: an input the data does not need gets driven toward irrelevance.

<MathBlock id="6.71" name="ARD covariance function">

$$k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \theta_0\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\eta_i(x_i - x_i')^2\right)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="One informative input, one pure-noise input: the marginal likelihood pushes the noise input's length scale to the edge of the search grid." width="wide">
  <ARDLengthScales client:visible />
</Figure>

Squashing a latent Gaussian process through a sigmoid turns it into a distribution over
class probabilities:

<MathBlock id="6.73" name="Bernoulli likelihood from a sigmoid">

$$p(t\mid a) = \sigma(a)^{t}(1-\sigma(a))^{1-t}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="A latent sample above, the same sample after the sigmoid below: every zero crossing becomes a 0.5 crossing." width="wide">
  <GPClassificationSquash client:visible />
</Figure>

A Laplace approximation to the posterior over the latent function gives a predictive mean
and variance in the same shape as regression's:

<MathBlock id="6.87" name="classification predictive mean">

$$\mathbb{E}[a_{N+1}\mid \mathbf{t}_N] = \mathbf{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{t}_N - \boldsymbol{\sigma}_N)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="6.88" name="classification predictive variance">

$$\mathrm{var}[a_{N+1}\mid \mathbf{t}_N] = c - \mathbf{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{W}_N^{-1} + \mathbf{C}_N)^{-1}\mathbf{k}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Two classes, an RBF kernel, the resulting predictive-probability field and its 0.5 boundary." source="6.12" width="wide">
  <ClassificationDecisionBoundary client:visible />
</Figure>

## Worked example

Five points, $\theta_0 = 1$, $\theta_1 = 4$, PRML's own Figure 6.5 setting, $\beta = 25$.

<Figure caption="The posterior mean and band this setting produces, with the two queried points marked." source="6.8">
  <GPRegressionWorkedNumbers client:visible />
</Figure>

At $x = 0.5$, inside the training range, the predictive variance is $0.058$; at $x = 1.2$,
past every training point, it is $0.270$, nearly five times as large, entirely from 6.67's
second term growing once no observation is nearby to shrink it.

## Recall

<Recall question="What does a Gaussian process specify that a weight-space model does not need to?" concepts={["gaussian-process"]}>
Nothing extra: 6.54 shows a weight prior already induces one. A Gaussian process just makes
the covariance function primary and drops the weights, which lets the kernel be anything
positive semi-definite, not only one built from a finite basis.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does a Gaussian kernel on closely spaced points need a jitter term?" concepts={["gaussian-process"]}>
Two nearly identical inputs make two rows of the Gram matrix nearly identical too, so the
matrix approaches singular. Adding a small constant to the diagonal keeps it factorisable.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does automatic relevance determination actually optimise?" concepts={["automatic-relevance-determination"]}>
A separate length scale per input dimension, fit by maximising the marginal likelihood.
An input the data does not need gets a length scale the likelihood keeps growing.
</Recall>

<Recall question="How does GP classification turn a real-valued process into class probabilities?" concepts={["gp-classification"]}>
By squashing a latent Gaussian process through a sigmoid (6.73), then approximating the
resulting non-Gaussian posterior, most simply with a Laplace approximation.
</Recall>
