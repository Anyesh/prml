## Intuition

The number of hidden units sets a network's raw capacity, but capacity is a blunt
instrument: too few and nothing fits, too many and everything fits, including the noise.
What actually needs controlling is effective complexity, and three quite different levers
reach it without ever touching the unit count.

Weight decay pulls every weight toward zero, so large, wild coefficients cost more than
small, cautious ones. Early stopping never changes the error function at all; it simply
quits training before the network has had time to memorise its particular sample of noise.
And when the real problem is a known invariance, rotate a digit, shift a signal, the answer
is neither: penalise the network directly for changing its output along that one direction,
because more data pulled from the same family will not teach the same lesson nearly as
directly.

## The math

Adding a penalty proportional to the weights themselves keeps everything else unchanged:

<MathBlock id="5.112" name="weight decay">

$$\tilde{E}(\mathbf{w}) = E(\mathbf{w}) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{w}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The same noisy fit at three decay strengths: the undecayed curve chases every point, and the wildest curve tames as λ grows." width="wide">
  <WeightDecayShrinkage client:visible />
</Figure>

For a known transformation $s(\mathbf{x}, \xi)$ (a rotation angle, a shift), its tangent
vector is how the input moves as $\xi$ leaves zero:

<MathBlock id="5.125" name="tangent vector">

$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \left.\frac{\partial s(\mathbf{x}, \xi)}{\partial \xi}\right|_{\xi=0}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="A shifted curve against the first-order prediction its tangent vector makes. The two nearly coincide for a small shift, which is exactly what makes the tangent vector a useful local stand-in for the whole transformation." width="wide">
  <TangentVectorConcept client:visible />
</Figure>

Penalising the output's Jacobian dotted with $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ discourages exactly the
sensitivity an invariance says should not exist:

<MathBlock id="5.128" name="tangent propagation penalty">

$$\Omega = \frac{1}{2}\sum_n \sum_k \left(\sum_i J_{nki}\tau_{ni}\right)^2$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

An alternative to penalising sensitivity directly: manufacture more training examples by
applying the transformation itself, here a small shift, and let ordinary training absorb
the invariance from the data.

<Figure caption="Average sensitivity to a shift of 0.1, before and after training includes shifted copies of every point. Five different initial weight seeds all show the same reduction." width="wide">
  <AugmentationReducesSensitivity client:visible />
</Figure>

Averaged over four training points, augmenting with shifted copies cuts mean sensitivity by
roughly a tenth compared to training on the unshifted points alone, at the cost of three
times as many training examples for the same information.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why can weight decay and early stopping often substitute for each other?" concepts={["weight-decay", "early-stopping"]}>
Both limit how far training moves weights from a small starting point: decay by directly
penalising their size, stopping by cutting the number of steps available to grow them.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does a tangent vector represent, concretely?" concepts={["tangent-propagation"]}>
The direction an input moves, to first order, as a known transformation's parameter leaves
zero: a rotation's tangent vector at angle zero, a shift's at offset zero.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why prefer a tangent-propagation penalty over training on transformed copies of the data?" concepts={["tangent-propagation"]}>
The penalty targets the exact direction the invariance describes directly, in one pass,
where augmented copies only teach it indirectly and need many examples to approximate the
same constraint.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does an untrained network's error keep falling on the training set even after validation error turns back up?" concepts={["early-stopping"]}>
Nothing stops the network from continuing to fit the training sample's specific noise; that
noise is not shared with the validation set, so fitting it more only pushes validation error
up.
</Recall>
