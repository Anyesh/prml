## Intuition

The gradient says which way is downhill. It says nothing about how far downhill goes before
the ground curves back up, or whether that curve is the same in every direction.

The Hessian answers exactly that: one number per pair of weights, describing how sharply
the error bends along each combination of them. Some directions turn out pinned down hard
by the data, changing the error fast; others are almost flat, so a wide range of values
along them costs almost nothing.

Getting the exact Hessian costs one more backward pass than the gradient did, extending the
same trick from section 5.3. But a much cheaper stand-in exists, built from pieces the
gradient already computed, and it is exact precisely where it matters least to be: right at
a good fit.

## The math

Restrict to one output unit and sum-of-squares error:

<MathBlock id="5.82" name="single-output sum-of-squares error">

$$E = \frac{1}{2}\sum_n (y_n - t_n)^2$$

</MathBlock>

Differentiating twice splits the exact Hessian into two pieces:

<MathBlock id="5.83" name="exact Hessian split into two terms">

$$\mathbf{H} = \sum_n \nabla y_n \nabla y_n^{\mathrm{T}} + \sum_n (y_n - t_n)\nabla\nabla y_n$$

</MathBlock>

The second term is weighted by the residual itself, so dropping it is exact wherever the fit
already is:

<MathBlock id="5.84" name="outer-product approximation">

$$\mathbf{H} \approx \sum_n \mathbf{b}_n \mathbf{b}_n^{\mathrm{T}}, \qquad \mathbf{b}_n = \nabla y_n$$

</MathBlock>

At a minimum, the Hessian's eigenvectors give the axes a quadratic bowl bends along:

<MathBlock id="5.33" name="Hessian eigenvalue equation">

$$\mathbf{H}\mathbf{u}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{u}_i$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

The same trained network, all thirteen weights this time, gives thirteen eigenvalues rather
than the two the ellipse above showed. With four data points and thirteen weights, the fit
is a near-exact interpolation, so several eigenvalues sit orders of magnitude below the
largest.

<Figure caption="Eigenvalues of the exact Hessian, sorted by magnitude on a log scale; red bars mark a negative sign. The smallest are close enough to zero that their sign is not meaningful, only their size." width="wide">
  <EigenvalueSpectrum client:visible />
</Figure>

Those near-zero directions are where thirteen weights fit four points: the data leaves them
almost entirely undetermined. The outer-product approximation gets the large end of the
spectrum right first, since it is built from $\nabla y_n$, largest exactly where the output
is most sensitive to a weight, the same directions the exact Hessian pins down hardest.

## Recall

<Recall question="What does a Hessian eigenvalue tell you that the gradient alone does not?" concepts={["hessian-matrix"]}>
How sharply the error curves along that eigenvector's direction: a large eigenvalue means
that direction is tightly pinned by the data, a small one means it is nearly free.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Which term does the outer-product approximation drop, and why is dropping it safe near a good fit?" concepts={["outer-product-approximation"]}>
It drops the term weighted by the residual (y − t) in 5.83. Near a good fit the residuals
are small, so that term is small too, and the remaining sum of outer products is nearly all
of the true Hessian.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is exactHessian's extra cost only one more backward pass, not a whole separate algorithm?" concepts={["hessian-matrix"]}>
It reuses backpropagation's own forward and backward recursions, differentiated once more
in a chosen direction, rather than perturbing pairs of weights and re-running the network.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the outer-product approximation guarantee that the exact Hessian does not?" concepts={["outer-product-approximation"]}>
It is a sum of outer products, so it is always positive semi-definite, even far from a
minimum where the exact Hessian can have negative eigenvalues.
</Recall>
