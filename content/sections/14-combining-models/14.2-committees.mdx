## Intuition

Fit the same flexible model to a hundred different samples of the same process and the
fits scatter: that scatter is variance, and it is pure waste, since it carries no signal
about the truth. Average the hundred fits and the waste tends to cancel, because each
fit's mistake points in whatever direction its own sample's noise happened to push it.

A committee turns that observation into a procedure. Fit several models independently and
average their predictions. Nothing about any single member has to be good, only that their
errors disagree with each other, so that averaging leaves the shared signal and cancels the
private noise.

The catch is the same one chapter 3 raised and set aside: cancelling requires several
datasets, and a real problem hands over exactly one. The fix reused here is the bootstrap,
resampling that one dataset with replacement to manufacture the variability a committee
needs to have anything to cancel.

## The math

A committee of $M$ members averages their predictions:

<MathBlock id="14.7" name="committee prediction">

$$y_{\text{COM}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}y_m(\mathbf{x})$$

</MathBlock>

Writing each member as the truth plus its own error, $y_m = h + \epsilon_m$, the members'
own average error is

<MathBlock id="14.10" name="average member error">

$$E_{\text{AV}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[\epsilon_m(\mathbf{x})^2\big]$$

</MathBlock>

while the committee's error uses the *average* error, not the average of squared errors:

<MathBlock id="14.11" name="committee error">

$$E_{\text{COM}} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\epsilon_m(\mathbf{x})\right)^2\right]$$

</MathBlock>

If every $\epsilon_m$ has zero mean and the errors are pairwise uncorrelated, the
cross-terms vanish and

<MathBlock id="14.14" name="committee error under uncorrelated mistakes">

$$E_{\text{COM}} = \frac{1}{M}E_{\text{AV}}$$

</MathBlock>

Real errors correlate at some level $\rho$, and the ratio degrades from $1/M$ to
$\rho + (1-\rho)/M$: a floor at $\rho$ that no amount of averaging removes.

## Worked example

<Figure caption="Squared error at each test point for 20 bootstrap members: their mean error against the committee's own. The committee curve sits at or below the member curve everywhere, never above it." width="wide">
  <PointwiseErrorComparison client:visible />
</Figure>

At $M = 20$ on this dataset, the committee's error is below the members' average error at
every one of the 50 test points plotted, matching 14.11's guarantee $E_{\text{COM}} \leq
E_{\text{AV}}$: convexity of the square means the average-then-square can never exceed the
square-then-average. Whether the gap reaches 14.14's factor of $1/M \approx 0.05$ depends
entirely on how correlated the 20 fits turned out to be, which the widget's right panel
shows directly.

## Recall

<Recall question="What has to be true of the members' errors for a committee to reach the full factor-of-M reduction in 14.14?" concepts={["committee"]}>
Zero mean and pairwise uncorrelated. Averaging turns the cross terms in 14.11 to zero, and
only the diagonal terms of 14.10 survive, scaled by $1/M$.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does bagging need bootstrap resamples rather than the one dataset used directly M times?" concepts={["bootstrap-aggregating"]}>
Fitting the same model to the same data M times gives M identical fits with nothing to
cancel. Resampling with replacement is what manufactures the disagreement a committee
needs, out of a single dataset.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Can a committee's error ever exceed the members' own average error?" concepts={["committee"]}>
No. $E_{\text{COM}} \leq E_{\text{AV}}$ always, because squaring is convex: the square of
an average of errors cannot exceed the average of their squares.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does the widget's right-hand gap rarely approach the naive 1/M line?" concepts={["committee", "bias-variance-tradeoff"]}>
Bootstrap resamples of one dataset overlap heavily, so the members' errors are correlated
rather than independent, and the achievable ratio sits nearer $\rho$ than $1/M$.
</Recall>
