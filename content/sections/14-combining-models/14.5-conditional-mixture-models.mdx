## Intuition

A decision tree's split is a hard rule: one region, one model, no blending. Soften that
rule and the tree's spirit survives in a form chapter 9 already built the machinery for,
a mixture, except the components are now regressions or classifiers instead of unconditional
densities.

<Figure caption="Data that switches which line generated it partway through. A single regression, fit to everything at once, cannot represent either half well." width="wide">
  <SingleLineVsData client:visible />
</Figure>

The plainest version keeps a fixed mixing weight per component, learned once for the whole
input space. That already beats a single model on data like this, but it still assigns
real probability to both lines everywhere, since nothing about the mixture knows *where*
each line applies. The fix is to let the mixing coefficients themselves be functions of the
input: a gate that learns the boundary between regimes, turning the tree's hard split into
a trainable, probabilistic one.

## The math

A mixture of $K$ linear regressions sharing one noise precision $\beta$:

<MathBlock id="14.34" name="mixture of linear regression models">

$$p(t\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}\big(t\mid\mathbf{w}_k^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}, \beta^{-1}\big)$$

</MathBlock>

Fitting it by EM needs exactly the responsibility chapter 9 already defines:

<MathBlock id="14.37" name="responsibility">

$$\gamma_{nk} = \frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}\big(t_n\mid\mathbf{w}_k^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}_n,\beta^{-1}\big)}{\sum_j\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}\big(t_n\mid\mathbf{w}_j^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}_n,\beta^{-1}\big)}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The converged mixture's predictive density: bimodal at every x, because a fixed-coefficient mixture never stops entertaining both lines everywhere." width="wide">
  <FixedMixturePredictiveDensity client:visible />
</Figure>

and an M step that is weighted least squares, responsibility standing in for a per-point
precision:

<MathBlock id="14.42" name="weighted normal equations">

$$\mathbf{w}_k = \big(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{R}_k\boldsymbol{\Phi}\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{R}_k\mathbf{t}, \qquad \mathbf{R}_k = \operatorname{diag}(\gamma_{nk})$$

</MathBlock>

A mixture of logistic models is the same story with a Bernoulli component in place of the
Gaussian. Letting the mixing coefficients depend on $\mathbf{x}$ turns either into a mixture
of experts:

<MathBlock id="14.53" name="mixture of experts">

$$p(t\mid\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k(\mathbf{x})\,p_k(t\mid\mathbf{x})$$

</MathBlock>

where the gating function $\pi_k(\mathbf{x})$ is fit the same way any posterior class
probability is: as a softmax, or for two components a single logistic unit, regressed onto
the responsibilities instead of onto hard labels.

On the two-line regression set, the converged mixture reaches a log-likelihood of $6.7$
against $-41.3$ for the best single line: the same qualitative gap the book reports for its
own two-line example, here even wider because the two true lines cross more sharply. On the
two-class set above, a single logistic model reaches $-23.4$; the mixture of two reaches
$-21.8$, a smaller but real gain, because these classes overlap enough that no split, hard
or soft, separates them perfectly.

## Recall

<Recall question="What does a fixed-coefficient mixture of linear regressions get wrong that a mixture of experts fixes?" concepts={["mixture-of-experts"]}>
Its predictive density stays multimodal everywhere, since the mixing coefficients cannot
favour one component in the regions where only that component actually applies. A gate
that depends on the input can.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What is the M step for wk in a mixture of linear regressions, and why does it not have a closed form for a mixture of logistic models?" concepts={["conditional-mixture"]}>
It is weighted least squares, 14.42, because each component's log-likelihood is quadratic
in wk. A logistic component's log-likelihood is not quadratic in wk, so its M step needs
an iterative solve, IRLS, instead.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does a gating network's target look like when it is trained, and how is that different from an ordinary classifier's target?" concepts={["gating-network"]}>
A responsibility in [0, 1] rather than a hard 0/1 label. The same cross-entropy fitting
procedure applies to both, since it never assumed the target was binary.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can a hierarchical mixture of experts be described as a probabilistic decision tree?" concepts={["mixture-of-experts", "decision-tree"]}>
Its gates play the role of a tree's splits, except soft and input-dependent rather than a
single hard threshold, and its experts play the role of the tree's per-region models.
</Recall>
