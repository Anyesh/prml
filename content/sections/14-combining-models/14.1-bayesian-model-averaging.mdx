## Intuition

Two rival explanations could fit a dataset, say a mixture of Gaussians against a mixture
of Cauchy distributions. There are two structurally different ways to hedge between them,
and both get called "combining models," which is exactly why the distinction below keeps
getting lost.

<Figure caption="Two candidate shapes and a handful of points. Nothing here says yet whether one shape produced all of them or each point picked its own." source="14.1">
  <CandidateDensities client:visible />
</Figure>

One way assumes a single explanation generated everything, and stays uncertain only about
which. Every point then answers the same question, so points accumulate into a verdict:
given enough data, one explanation wins outright and the other's share vanishes. That is
Bayesian model averaging.

The other way assumes different points came from different components inside one model,
the same idea already used to build a mixture of Gaussians. No point ever settles anything
for the whole dataset, because there was never a single dataset-wide question to settle.
More data sharpens the estimate of the mix. It does not sharpen the mix itself.

## The math

Start from a mixture with a latent indicator $z$ choosing which component made a point:

<MathBlock id="14.2" name="joint over an observation and its latent">

$$p(\mathbf{x}, z)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="14.3" name="marginal by summing out the latent">

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{z}p(\mathbf{x}, z)$$

</MathBlock>

which for a Gaussian mixture is the familiar

<MathBlock id="14.4" name="Gaussian mixture density">

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)$$

</MathBlock>

Every data point $\mathbf{x}_n$ carries its own latent $z_n$, so a whole dataset's
probability keeps one summation per point:

<MathBlock id="14.5" name="dataset marginal with one latent per point">

$$p(\mathbf{X}) = \prod_{n=1}^{N}p(\mathbf{x}_n) = \prod_{n=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{z_n}p(\mathbf{x}_n, z_n)\right)$$

</MathBlock>

Now index whole *models* by $h$, each with its own parameters, and put a prior over $h$
instead of over each point's component:

<MathBlock id="14.6" name="Bayesian model averaging">

$$p(\mathbf{X}) = \sum_{h=1}^{H}p(\mathbf{X}\mid h)p(h)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The same prior, turned into a posterior by 20 points. One bar keeps essentially all the mass; equation 14.6 has already picked a winner." width="wide">
  <PosteriorOverModels client:visible />
</Figure>

There is exactly one sum over $z_n$ per data point in 14.5, but exactly one sum over $h$
for the *entire* dataset in 14.6. That single sum, shared by every point, is the whole
difference: it is why $h$'s posterior can concentrate while $z_n$'s cannot.

<Figure caption="The same two candidate shapes as mixture components instead of rival models. Mean responsibility of component 0 barely moves between 5 and 20 points, unlike the posterior above." width="wide">

</Figure>

## Worked example

<Figure caption="Twenty points from a genuine 50/50 mixture. Colour marks which component actually wrote each point; height is that component's computed responsibility for it." width="wide">
  <ResponsibilitySpread client:visible />
</Figure>

With equal priors and the two unit-variance Gaussians centred at $-1.2$ and $1.2$, a single
point at $x = 0$ splits its responsibility exactly $0.5$ / $0.5$, since both densities agree
there by symmetry. Averaged over 20 draws from the true mixture, the mean responsibility of
component 0 computed here is close to the true $0.5$ mixing weight, and it stays that close
whether 5 points are used or all 20.

Contrast the posterior over models from the math block: with 20 points drawn from a single
true generator, that posterior is no longer close to the $0.5$ prior it started from. One
quantity moved off its starting value under more data; the other did not, because it was
never trying to.

## Recall

<Recall question="What does Bayesian model averaging assume about who generated the dataset?" concepts={["bayesian-model-averaging"]}>
That one model, unknown but fixed, generated every point. The sum in 14.6 is over that
uncertainty, not over per-point origin.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does a mixture's inferred proportions not sharpen toward one component as data grows, the way a model posterior does?" concepts={["model-combination"]}>
Because a mixture never claimed a single component produced the dataset. Each point keeps
its own responsibility, computed from where it sits, not from how many other points exist.
</Recall>

<Recall question="In equations 14.5 and 14.6, which sum is per point and which is shared across the whole dataset?" concepts={["bayesian-model-averaging", "model-combination"]}>
14.5's sum over $z_n$ is repeated once per point. 14.6's sum over $h$ appears exactly once,
covering every point at once, and that single shared sum is what lets it concentrate.
</Recall>

<Recall question="A mixture of Gaussians and Bayesian model averaging over {Gaussian mixture, Cauchy mixture} both fit the description 'weighted combination of densities.' What structural fact tells them apart?" concepts={["model-combination", "bayesian-model-averaging"]}>
Where the latent variable lives. One latent per data point marks a mixture; one latent for
the entire dataset, chosen once, marks Bayesian model averaging.
</Recall>
