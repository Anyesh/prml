## Intuition

A committee fits its members independently and hopes their mistakes disagree. Boosting
does not hope. It looks at exactly which points the last member got wrong and hands the
next member a dataset that has been reweighted to make those points impossible to ignore.

<Figure caption="One decision stump, a single threshold on one axis, on data that overlaps too much for any threshold to separate cleanly. This is a weak learner: better than chance, nowhere near enough on its own." source="14.1">
  <WeakLearnerIllustration client:visible />
</Figure>

Each member in the sequence is typically tiny, often a single threshold called a decision
stump. What carries the weight is the sequence: points that keep getting missed accumulate
weight round after round until some member is forced to address them, and every member's
final vote is scaled by how well it did on the weights it was actually trained on.

Nothing about this needs a committee's assumption that errors are already uncorrelated.
Boosting manufactures the disagreement on purpose, which is why it can reach further than
bagging does.

## The math

Each round fits a classifier minimizing the currently weighted error:

<MathBlock id="14.15" name="weighted error of a base classifier">

$$J_m = \sum_{n=1}^{N}w_n^{(m)}\,\mathbb{I}(y_m(\mathbf{x}_n) \neq t_n)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="14.16" name="normalized weighted error epsilon">

$$\epsilon_m = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_n^{(m)}\,\mathbb{I}(y_m(\mathbf{x}_n)\neq t_n)}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_n^{(m)}}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="14.17" name="classifier vote weight alpha">

$$\alpha_m = \ln\left(\frac{1-\epsilon_m}{\epsilon_m}\right)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="14.18" name="data weight update">

$$w_n^{(m+1)} = w_n^{(m)}\exp\{\alpha_m\,\mathbb{I}(y_m(\mathbf{x}_n)\neq t_n)\}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="14.19" name="combined AdaBoost classifier">

$$Y_M(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{sign}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_m y_m(\mathbf{x})\right)$$

</MathBlock>

A correctly classified point's weight is untouched; a misclassified one is scaled up by
$e^{\alpha_m}$, more so for a classifier that was otherwise accurate. Friedman et al. showed
this whole recipe drops out of minimizing one error function a term at a time:

<MathBlock id="14.20" name="exponential error function">

$$E = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\exp\{-t_nf_m(\mathbf{x}_n)\}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The exponential error next to cross-entropy, hinge, and misclassification error, all as functions of the margin z = t times y(x). Only the exponential curve keeps climbing this steeply for a confident wrong answer." width="wide">
  <ErrorFunctionComparison client:visible />
</Figure>

Its population minimizer is exactly half the log-odds:

<MathBlock id="14.27" name="expected exponential error">

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},t}\big[\exp\{-ty(\mathbf{x})\}\big]$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="14.28" name="minimizer is half the log-odds">

$$y(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{p(t=1\mid\mathbf{x})}{p(t=-1\mid\mathbf{x})}\right)$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

<Figure caption="Every point's margin after one round against after all six. The fraction with a positive margin rises from 27 of 30 to 29 of 30, and the ones still near zero are the persistently hard points." width="wide">
  <MarginDistributionShift client:visible />
</Figure>

On this 30-point set, round 1's stump alone has weighted error $\epsilon_1 = 0.10$ and vote
weight $\alpha_1 = 2.20$; by round 6, $\epsilon_6$ has risen to $0.27$ as easy points stop
contributing new information, so $\alpha_6$ falls to $1.01$. The combined vote in 14.19
still improves on any single round, correctly classifying 29 of the 30 training points
against 27 after round 1 alone, and the one point still wrong is exactly the kind 14.18
keeps reweighting: it has stayed on the wrong side of every stump so far.

The same additive-model idea covers regression once the exponential error is swapped for
one that fits residuals, but the swap matters: squared residual error punishes an outlier
quadratically, absolute error only linearly.

<Figure caption="Squared error grows faster than absolute error for the same large residual, which is why regression boosting built on absolute deviation is the more outlier-robust choice." width="wide">
  <RegressionBoostingComparison client:visible />
</Figure>

## Recall

<Recall question="What changes between rounds in AdaBoost, and what stays fixed?" concepts={["adaboost"]}>
The data weights change every round, growing on points the previous classifier missed.
The base learning procedure itself, whatever fits a classifier to weighted data, stays
fixed throughout.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does a more accurate base classifier get a larger alpha in 14.17?" concepts={["adaboost"]}>
Alpha is $\ln((1-\epsilon)/\epsilon)$, increasing as $\epsilon$ falls. A classifier with
small weighted error votes with more weight in the final sum.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What loss is AdaBoost implicitly minimizing, one term at a time?" concepts={["exponential-error"]}>
The exponential error of 14.20, summed over training points, with each new base classifier
and its weight chosen to minimize the next unaccounted-for term.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is the exponential error less robust to outliers than cross-entropy?" concepts={["exponential-error"]}>
For a confidently wrong prediction, cross-entropy grows linearly in the margin's
magnitude while the exponential error grows exponentially, so one badly mislabelled point
can dominate every later round.
</Recall>
