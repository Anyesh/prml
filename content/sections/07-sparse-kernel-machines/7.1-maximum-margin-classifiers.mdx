## Intuition

A straight boundary that separates two classes is rarely unique: many lines pass between two clusters of points. The perceptron from chapter four finds one and stops, and which one depends on where it started and the order it saw the data, not on which boundary generalises best.

Support vector machines settle the choice with one principle: pick the boundary sitting as far as possible from the nearest point of either class. That distance is the margin. Once fixed, only the points the boundary just barely clears turn out to matter; every other point could be deleted without moving the answer. That is the chapter's title: the fitted machine remembers a sparse handful of support vectors and nothing else.

Real data rarely separates cleanly, so a softened version lets a few points sit on the wrong side for a price. The same idea extends to predicting a number instead of a label, and to more than two classes, though neither extension is as clean as the original.

## The math

Restrict to a linear $y(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}) + b$ and assume the classes separate. A correctly classified point sits at distance $t_n y(\mathbf{x}_n)/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ from the boundary, so the margin is maximised by

<MathBlock id="7.3" name="margin objective">

$$\arg\max_{\mathbf{w}, b}\left\{\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\min_n\big[t_n(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n)+b)\big]\right\}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The margin as a distance: the dashed segment is |y(x)|/‖w‖ from the point to y(x) = 0." width="wide">
  <MarginAsDistance client:visible />
</Figure>

Fixing the closest point at distance $1/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ by rescaling turns this into a quadratic program:

<MathBlock id="7.6" name="canonical quadratic program">

$$\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}, b} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.}\quad t_n(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n)+b) \geqslant 1$$

</MathBlock>

One multiplier $a_n$ per constraint, eliminated against $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$, gives a dual depending on the data only through a kernel:

<MathBlock id="7.10" name="dual Lagrangian">

$$L(\mathbf{a}) = \sum_n a_n - \frac{1}{2}\sum_n\sum_m a_n a_m t_n t_m k(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x}_m)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="7.13" name="kernel decision function">

$$y(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_n a_n t_n k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_n) + b$$

</MathBlock>

KKT forces $a_n\{t_ny(\mathbf{x}_n) - 1\} = 0$:

<MathBlock id="7.16" name="KKT complementary slackness">

$$a_n\big(t_n y(\mathbf{x}_n) - 1\big) = 0$$

</MathBlock>

so a point either sits exactly on the margin or drops out entirely: sparsity falls out of the optimum rather than being imposed on it. Overlapping classes need slack $\xi_n$, minimising

<MathBlock id="7.21" name="soft-margin primal objective">

$$C\sum_n \xi_n + \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$$

</MathBlock>

whose dual is unchanged except for a box on every multiplier:

<MathBlock id="7.33" name="box constraint">

$$0 \leqslant a_n \leqslant C$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Overlapping classes at C = 1: rings are support vectors, triangles sit past the wrong margin." width="wide">
  <SlackVariablesOverlap client:visible />
</Figure>

The same fit minimises the hinge loss against a quadratic regulariser:

<MathBlock id="7.45" name="hinge loss">

$$E_{\mathrm{SV}}(t_ny_n) = [1 - t_ny_n]_+$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Hinge, rescaled logistic, squared and misclassification error on one axis." width="wide">
  <HingeLossComparison client:visible />
</Figure>

against logistic regression's, rescaled to match at the origin:

<MathBlock id="7.48" name="rescaled logistic error">

$$E_{\mathrm{LR}}(t_ny_n) = \ln\big(1+\exp(-t_ny_n)\big)/\ln 2$$

</MathBlock>

Logistic regression's error never flattens to zero, so it never produces a sparse solution. K classes have no equally clean answer: the usual fix trains K one-versus-rest machines and assigns

<MathBlock id="7.49" name="one-versus-rest max rule">

$$y(\mathbf{x}) = \max_k y_k(\mathbf{x})$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Three one-versus-rest SVMs: red is contested by more than one, grey by none." width="wide">
  <MulticlassAmbiguitySvm client:visible />
</Figure>

Regression keeps a prediction free of penalty inside a tolerance:

<MathBlock id="7.51" name="epsilon-insensitive error">

$$E_\epsilon(y(\mathbf{x})-t) = \max(0, |y(\mathbf{x})-t| - \epsilon)$$

</MathBlock>

whose dual again reduces to a box-constrained quadratic program over two multipliers per point:

<MathBlock id="7.61" name="support vector regression dual">

$$L(\mathbf{a}, \hat{\mathbf{a}}) = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}(a_n-\hat a_n)(a_m-\hat a_m)k_{nm} - \epsilon\sum_n(a_n+\hat a_n) + \sum_n(a_n-\hat a_n)t_n$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

Six points at $C=10$ with a linear kernel: class $+1$ at $(3,2),(4,3.5),(3.5,0.8),(5,2.5),(2.5,3.2)$, class $-1$ at their mirror images across the origin's neighbourhood, $(-1,-2),(-2.2,-3),(-0.3,-3.5),(-2.8,-1.2),(-3.5,-0.4)$. Solving 7.10 under 7.33 gives exactly two nonzero multipliers, both $a=0.0712$, on $(3.5,0.8)$ and $(-2.2,-3)$: every other point could vanish and $y(\mathbf{x})$ would not change.

<Figure caption="Support-vector count against C on an overlapping set: small C keeps almost every point active; large C prunes down to the true margin." width="wide">

</Figure>

Weighting $\mathbf{w}=\sum a_nt_n\mathbf{x}_n$ gives $\mathbf{w}\approx(0.406, 0.271)$ and a margin width $2/\|\mathbf{w}\|\approx4.11$. Solving 7.51's regression analogue on a noisy sine with $\epsilon=0.1$ keeps most points inside the tube for free:

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does a support vector machine end up remembering only a few training points?" concepts={["support-vector"]}>
KKT complementary slackness (7.16) forces every multiplier to be either zero or attached to a point sitting exactly on the margin. Zero-multiplier points never appear in the decision function.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the parameter C actually trade off?" concepts={["soft-margin"]}>
Margin width against the total slack allowed. Small C tolerates many violations and keeps a wide margin; large C approaches the hard-margin limit and can overfit outliers.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does logistic regression never produce a sparse fit the way the hinge loss does?" concepts={["hinge-loss"]}>
The hinge loss is exactly zero past the margin, so points comfortably correct contribute nothing to the gradient. The logistic error keeps a small nonzero slope everywhere, so every point stays involved.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does sequential minimal optimisation update exactly two multipliers at a time rather than one?" concepts={["sequential-minimal-optimisation"]}>
The equality constraint ties every multiplier to the rest; changing just one would break it. Two can be updated together while holding their signed sum fixed, and that two-variable problem has a closed form.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What replaces C when a regression tube is fit instead of a classification margin?" concepts={["support-vector-regression"]}>
Epsilon, the width of the zero-cost tube, alongside C. A point inside the tube costs nothing regardless of C; only points outside it become support vectors.
</Recall>
