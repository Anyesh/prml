## Intuition

A coin has one unknown number: how often it lands heads. Flip it three times and see three
heads, and the maximum-likelihood answer is that it always will. Nothing in the estimate
carries a sense of how little evidence it rests on.

A prior belief fixes that, but only if updating it stays cheap. Otherwise every new flip
means re-deriving a distribution from scratch, which is not something you can do by hand or
afford in a running system. The beta distribution is the belief that survives its own
updating: multiply it by a single flip's evidence and what comes out is another beta,
just with two counters nudged. That is why this one distribution, out of every possible
prior, is the one worth knowing first.

## The math

A single trial with outcome $x \in \{0, 1\}$ is Bernoulli:

<MathBlock id="2.1" name="Bernoulli distribution">

$$p(x=1 \mid \mu) = \mu$$

</MathBlock>

Its maximum-likelihood estimate from $N$ trials is the sample mean,

<MathBlock id="2.7" name="maximum likelihood estimate">

$$\mu_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} x_n$$

</MathBlock>

which is exactly the count of heads over trials, $m/N$, and exactly what fails with three
heads out of three below.

<Figure caption="ML certainty versus a Beta(2,2) prior's predictive probability, both computed from the same three flips." source="2.3">
  <MLThreeHeads client:visible />
</Figure>

A conjugate prior over $\mu$ closes the loop. The beta distribution,

<MathBlock id="2.13" name="beta distribution">

$$\mathrm{Beta}(\mu \mid a, b) = \frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}\mu^{a-1}(1-\mu)^{b-1}$$

</MathBlock>

has mean

<MathBlock id="2.15" name="beta mean">

$$\mathbb{E}[\mu] = \frac{a}{a+b}$$

</MathBlock>

and, multiplied by the Bernoulli likelihood of $m$ heads and $l$ tails, gives a posterior in
the same family:

<MathBlock id="2.18" name="beta posterior">

$$p(\mu \mid m, l, a, b) = \mathrm{Beta}(\mu \mid a+m, b+l)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Prior, the single-flip likelihood, and posterior on the same axes: multiplying a beta by a Bernoulli term never leaves the family." source="2.3">
  <SequentialPosteriorStaysBeta client:visible />
</Figure>

$a$ and $b$ behave as fictitious prior counts of heads and tails, which is why they add
directly. Averaging $\mu$ over that posterior gives the predictive probability of the next
flip:

<MathBlock id="2.20" name="predictive distribution">

$$p(x=1 \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{m+a}{m+a+l+b}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Beta(µ|a,b) for four hyperparameter pairs: a=b weights symmetrically, a≠b skews, and both above 1 pulls the mode off the boundary." source="2.2">
  <BetaShapes client:visible />
</Figure>

## Worked example

Prior Beta(2, 2), then a fair-looking run of 80 flips from a coin actually biased at
$\mu=0.7$, fed through equation 2.18 flip by flip.

<Figure caption="Posterior mean over 80 identical flips under a weak prior (worth one flip each way) against a strong one (worth 20)." source="2.12">
  <PosteriorMeanConverges client:visible />
</Figure>

The weak prior's posterior mean is within 0.05 of 0.7 after roughly ten flips. The strong
prior, equivalent to 40 fictitious observations split evenly, is still above 0.55 after
thirty and only closes in near the end of the run. Same data, same posterior *family*,
different speed, because the strength of a beta prior is literally how many observations it
takes to overrule.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does a single Beta(2,2) update always land on another beta distribution?" concepts={["conjugate-prior"]}>
Because the Bernoulli likelihood contributes factors of $\mu^{x}(1-\mu)^{1-x}$, which have
the same $\mu^{a-1}(1-\mu)^{b-1}$ shape the beta density already has. Multiplying just adds
exponents (2.18).
</Recall>

<Recall question="What do the hyperparameters a and b mean, concretely?" concepts={["beta-distribution"]}>
Fictitious prior counts of heads and tails. Observing $m$ real heads and $l$ real tails adds
directly: $a \to a+m$, $b \to b+l$.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is three heads out of three a genuine failure of maximum likelihood, not just a small-sample quirk?" concepts={["maximum-likelihood"]}>
Because $\mu_{\mathrm{ML}}=1$ asserts certainty from three trials, and nothing in the ML
estimate itself signals that this certainty is unwarranted. It takes a prior to express
"three flips is not enough to know that."
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the predictive distribution 2.20 reduce to as data accumulates?" concepts={["posterior-distribution"]}>
The maximum-likelihood estimate $m/N$, since $a$ and $b$ become negligible next to $m$ and
$l$. A confident-enough prior is just a claim that this limit has effectively been reached
already.
</Recall>
