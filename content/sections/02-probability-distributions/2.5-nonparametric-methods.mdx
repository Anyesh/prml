## Intuition

Every distribution in this chapter so far commits to a shape before seeing data: a
Gaussian, a beta, a mixture of a chosen number of components. That commitment is a real
risk. Real data is under no obligation to be unimodal, symmetric, or a mixture of any
particular count of anything.

Nonparametric methods sidestep the commitment by tying the model to the data itself: count
how many points fall near a query location, in a region whose size is the one thing you
choose. Shrink the region and the estimate chases every wrinkle in the sample, most of it
noise. Grow it and real structure gets smoothed away. There is no shape assumption doing the
picking, which is also why there is no shortcut around the trade-off itself.

## The math

A histogram assigns each bin's own share of the data to its own width:

<MathBlock id="2.241" name="histogram estimator">

$$p_i = \frac{n_i}{N\Delta_i}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Same 50 points, three bin widths: too fine catches noise, too coarse hides the second mode." source="2.24">
  <HistogramBinWidth client:visible />
</Figure>

Generalising: a region of volume $V$ around $x$ catching $K$ of $N$ points estimates the
density there directly,

<MathBlock id="2.246" name="density from a local count">

$$p(x) = \frac{K}{NV}$$

</MathBlock>

Fixing $V$ and letting $K$ vary with the data gives kernel density estimation; the smoothed,
Gaussian-kernel version replaces every bin edge with a soft bump per point:

<MathBlock id="2.250" name="Gaussian kernel estimator">

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{(2\pi h^2)^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-x_n\|^2}{2h^2}\right)$$

</MathBlock>

Fixing $K$ instead and growing $V$ until it holds exactly that many points gives K-nearest-
neighbours, the same 2.246 read the other way. Applied per class it gives a classifier:

<MathBlock id="2.253" name="class-conditional density">

$$p(x \mid \mathcal{C}_k) = \frac{K_k}{N_k V}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="2.254" name="unconditional density">

$$p(x) = \frac{K}{NV}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="2.256" name="KNN posterior">

$$p(\mathcal{C}_k \mid x) = \frac{K_k}{K}$$

</MathBlock>

</Figure>

## Worked example

<Figure caption="log10 of the bin count against dimension, at 3 bins per axis: exponential growth reads as a straight line here." source="1.6">
  <CurseOfDimensionalityBins client:visible />
</Figure>

Three bins per input dimension, ten dimensions: $3^{10} = 59{,}049$ bins. Almost every bin is
empty for any dataset smaller than tens of thousands of points, and this was the *gentle*
case, three bins rather than the ten or so a real histogram would want. K-nearest-neighbours
and kernel methods inherit the same growth: a fixed-radius neighbourhood in high dimensions
is nearly always empty too.

## Recall

<Recall question="What single relation unifies histograms, kernel density estimation, and K-nearest-neighbours?" concepts={["nonparametric-density-estimation"]}>
$p(x) = K/(NV)$ (2.246). Fixing $V$ and reading off $K$ gives kernel methods; fixing $K$ and
solving for $V$ gives K-nearest-neighbours; a histogram is the same idea with $V$ fixed to a
grid cell.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does a K-nearest-neighbour density estimate fail to be a genuine density?" concepts={["k-nearest-neighbours"]}>
Its integral over all space diverges: the volume needed to catch $K$ points never stops
growing far from the data, so the "density" there falls too slowly to integrate to a finite
total.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What is the real cost of the curse of dimensionality for these methods?" concepts={["curse-of-dimensionality"]}>
A neighbourhood or bin that would be well populated in one or two dimensions becomes almost
certainly empty once dimension grows, because covering a space with local structure costs
exponentially in its dimension.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does bandwidth or K actually trade off, and what is it a special case of?" concepts={["kernel-density-estimation"]}>
Variance against bias in the estimate, the same split section 3.2's bias-variance
decomposition names for model complexity generally. A smoothing parameter is a complexity
knob under another name.
</Recall>
