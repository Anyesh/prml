## Intuition

Nine ideas live in this section and every one answers the same question: what can you still
do in closed form once a Gaussian is not the whole story. Split a vector Gaussian in two and
both the leftover piece and the relationship between the pieces stay Gaussian. Chain a
Gaussian prior into a Gaussian observation and both the prediction and the update stay
Gaussian. Even the Gaussian's own blind spots, its light tails, its straight-line notion of
distance on a circle, its single bump, are each fixed by a distribution built from
Gaussians rather than by abandoning them.

That closure under so many operations is the actual reason the Gaussian is everywhere in
this book rather than a rival density. Chapter 3's predictive distribution, its sequential
updating, and its evidence are this section's results wearing a regression problem's
clothes.

## The math

The density and the shape of its contours:

<MathBlock id="2.43" name="multivariate Gaussian density">

$$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D/2}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right\}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="2.50" name="quadratic form in eigencoordinates">

$$\Delta^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The covariance's eigenvectors are the contour's axes; eigenvalues are the axis lengths squared." source="2.7">
  <EigenDecomposition client:visible />
</Figure>

**Conditioning and marginalising** (2.3.1-2.3.2). Split $\mathbf{x}$ into $\mathbf{x}_a,
\mathbf{x}_b$: the conditional stays Gaussian,

<MathBlock id="2.81" name="conditional mean">

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_{a|b} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_a + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ab}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{bb}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_b-\boldsymbol{\mu}_b)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="2.82" name="conditional covariance">

$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{a|b} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{aa} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ab}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{bb}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ba}$$

</MathBlock>

and the marginal is simply the corresponding block:

<MathBlock id="2.92" name="marginal mean and covariance">

$$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_a]=\boldsymbol{\mu}_a,\qquad \mathrm{cov}[\mathbf{x}_a]=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{aa}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Same joint density: a horizontal slice gives the conditional, dropping a coordinate gives the marginal." source="2.9">
  <PartitionAndConditional client:visible />
</Figure>

**The linear-Gaussian model** (2.3.3) chains a prior into a linear-Gaussian likelihood and
keeps both directions closed-form:

<MathBlock id="2.114" name="linear-Gaussian marginal">

$$p(\mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{b},\ \mathbf{L}^{-1}+\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{T}})$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="p(x) pushed through a linear-Gaussian map: the marginal variance is the map's own plus the observation noise." source="2.10">
  <LinearGaussianMarginalFigure client:visible />
</Figure>

<MathBlock id="2.116" name="linear-Gaussian posterior">

$$p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x}\mid \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\{\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b})+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\mu}\},\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Three observed y, one posterior variance: only the mean depends on what was seen." source="2.10">
  <LinearGaussianPosteriorFigure client:visible />
</Figure>

**Maximum likelihood** (2.3.4) is again the sample mean, but the covariance is biased low:

<MathBlock id="2.122" name="ML covariance estimate">

$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{ML}})(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{ML}})^{\mathrm{T}}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="2.124" name="bias of the ML covariance">

$$\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{ML}}] = \frac{N-1}{N}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Averaged over thousands of small datasets, the ML variance sits exactly on the (N-1)/N line, not on the truth." source="2.14">
  <MLVarianceBias client:visible />
</Figure>

**Sequential estimation** (2.3.5) updates the same estimate one point at a time, derived
from Robbins-Monro root-finding rather than rearranged algebra:

<MathBlock id="2.126" name="sequential mean update">

$$\mu_{\mathrm{ML}}^{(N)} = \mu_{\mathrm{ML}}^{(N-1)} + \frac{1}{N}\left(x_N-\mu_{\mathrm{ML}}^{(N-1)}\right)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Equation 2.126 applied one point at a time, converging on the generating mean without ever storing the data." source="2.11">
  <SequentialMeanConvergenceFigure client:visible />
</Figure>

**Bayesian inference for the Gaussian** (2.3.6) puts a conjugate prior on the mean, the
precision, or both. Known variance, unknown mean, precisions add:

<MathBlock id="2.142" name="posterior precision additivity">

$$\frac{1}{\sigma_N^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{N}{\sigma^2}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Prior and posterior over the mean after 0, 1, 2, and 10 observations, narrowing exactly as 2.142 predicts." source="2.12">
  <BayesianMeanNarrowing client:visible />
</Figure>

Known mean, unknown precision, a gamma prior updates its shape by half a count per point:

<MathBlock id="2.150" name="gamma posterior shape">

$$a_N = a_0 + \frac{N}{2}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Gamma posterior over a precision, four sample sizes, tightening around the true value." source="2.13">
  <BayesianPrecisionNarrowing client:visible />
</Figure>

**Student's t** (2.3.7) is what a Gaussian looks like once its own precision is uncertain
and integrated out, an infinite mixture over a gamma-distributed precision:

<MathBlock id="2.159" name="Student's t density">

$$\mathrm{St}(x\mid\mu,\lambda,\nu) = \frac{\Gamma(\nu/2+1/2)}{\Gamma(\nu/2)}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\pi\nu}\right)^{1/2}\left[1+\frac{\lambda(x-\mu)^2}{\nu}\right]^{-\nu/2-1/2}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Same variance, very different tails: t survives an outlier that would move a Gaussian fit substantially." source="2.16">
  <StudentTHeavyTail client:visible />
</Figure>

**Periodic variables** (2.3.8): the von Mises distribution is the Gaussian shape built to
close on itself,

<MathBlock id="2.179" name="von Mises density">

$$p(\theta\mid\theta_0,\kappa) = \frac{1}{2\pi I_0(\kappa)}\exp\{\kappa\cos(\theta-\theta_0)\}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Density as radial bulge on the unit circle: higher concentration, tighter bulge, always periodic." source="2.19">
  <VonMisesCircle client:visible />
</Figure>

**Mixtures of Gaussians** (2.3.9) sum weighted components to reach shapes no single Gaussian
can:

<MathBlock id="2.188" name="mixture of Gaussians">

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="One Gaussian fit by maximum likelihood sits in the valley between two real modes; the true two-component mixture does not." source="2.21">
  <GaussianMixtureFit client:visible />
</Figure>

## Worked example

<Figure caption="1° and 359° averaged the ordinary way land at 180°, on the wrong side of the circle entirely." source="2.17">
  <VonMisesNaiveMeanFails client:visible />
</Figure>

Two compass headings, 1° and 359°, both pointing almost due east. The arithmetic mean is
180°, due west: the worst possible answer. Fitting a von Mises distribution instead, via
`vonMisesFit`, recovers a mean direction within a degree of due east and a large
concentration, because it works with the headings as points on a circle rather than numbers
on a line. Nothing about the *data* was unusual; only the wrong distance function was.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does conditioning a Gaussian never need to touch an integral?" concepts={["gaussian-conditioning"]}>
Because the conditional's mean and covariance (2.81-2.82) come straight from the joint's own
mean and covariance blocks. What would be an integral for a general distribution is matrix
algebra here.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the linear-Gaussian model let you compute that plain conditioning cannot?" concepts={["linear-gaussian-model"]}>
The marginal and posterior when you only have p(x) and p(y|x) separately, as in Bayesian
linear regression's predictive distribution, without ever assembling the full joint over
(x, y).
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is the maximum-likelihood covariance estimate biased low, and by how much?" concepts={["maximum-likelihood"]}>
It measures spread around its own sample mean rather than the true one, which is always at
least as tight a fit. The bias is exact: (N-1)/N times the true covariance (2.124).
</Recall>

<Recall question="How does sequential estimation (2.126) differ from Bayesian sequential learning?" concepts={["sequential-maximum-likelihood"]}>
It updates one point estimate with no posterior at all, derived from Robbins-Monro
root-finding. Bayesian sequential learning instead keeps a full distribution and lets
today's posterior become tomorrow's prior.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What kind of uncertainty does Student's t add on top of a Gaussian?" concepts={["student-t-distribution"]}>
Uncertainty about the precision itself. Averaging a Gaussian over a gamma-distributed
precision (2.158) is what produces its heavier tails, not an arbitrary reshaping.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does an ordinary arithmetic mean fail for angles?" concepts={["von-mises-distribution"]}>
Because it depends on where zero is drawn on the circle, so 1° and 359° average to 180°
instead of to the direction they actually cluster near. A circular mean, via the resultant
vector, does not have this problem.
</Recall>
