## Intuition

A coin has one number to estimate. A die has six, and they cannot be estimated one at a
time, because they are tied together: raise the chance of rolling a six and something else
must give. The right object for "several tied-together probabilities" is not a list of
numbers but a point on a simplex, the flat surface where every coordinate is non-negative
and the whole vector sums to one.

The beta distribution priced belief over a point on a line segment. Its extension to a
simplex is the Dirichlet distribution, built the same way and for the same reason: so that
updating a belief about a die, or a mixture, or any set of competing categories, stays
closed-form addition rather than a fresh integral every time.

## The math

A single trial over $K$ categories is a 1-of-K vector,

<MathBlock id="2.25" name="1-of-K coding">

$$\mathbf{x} = (0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0)^{\mathrm{T}}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="One coordinate on, the rest off: category 3 of 6 as a 1-of-K vector." source="2.25">
  <OneOfKCoding client:visible />
</Figure>

with categorical density $p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \prod_k \mu_k^{x_k}$,

<MathBlock id="2.26" name="categorical distribution">

$$p(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \prod_{k=1}^{K} \mu_k^{x_k}$$

</MathBlock>

Summed over $N$ trials, the category counts $m_k$ are Multinomial,

<MathBlock id="2.34" name="multinomial distribution">

$$\mathrm{Mult}(m_1,\dots,m_K \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, N) = \binom{N}{m_1\,\cdots\,m_K}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mu_k^{m_k}$$

</MathBlock>

and maximum likelihood is again just the observed proportions,

<MathBlock id="2.33" name="multinomial ML estimate">

$$\mu_k^{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac{m_k}{N}$$

</MathBlock>

The Dirichlet distribution puts a prior over $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ itself,

<MathBlock id="2.38" name="Dirichlet distribution">

$$\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\mu}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\Gamma(\alpha_1)\cdots\Gamma(\alpha_K)}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mu_k^{\alpha_k-1}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The Dirichlet on the 2-simplex for three concentrations: below 1 pulls mass to the corners, above 1 pulls it to the centre." source="2.5">
  <DirichletCorners client:visible />
</Figure>

with $\alpha_0 = \sum_k \alpha_k$, and, as with the beta, observing counts $m_k$ just adds
them onto the concentrations:

<MathBlock id="2.41" name="Dirichlet posterior">

$$p(\boldsymbol{\mu}\mid\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\mu}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\mathbf{m})$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="A uniform Dirichlet(2,2,2) prior against its posterior after three categories are observed 5, 2, and 9 times." source="2.41">
  <MultinomialPosteriorUpdate client:visible />
</Figure>

Set one concentration far above the other two: the density collapses onto the edge of the
simplex nearest that category, the geometric picture of one outcome dominating a prior
belief before any data arrives.

## Worked example

A uniform Dirichlet(2, 2, 2) prior over three categories A, B, C, and 16 observations
landing 5, 2, and 9 times respectively (the figure above). Equation 2.41 gives posterior
Dirichlet(7, 4, 11).

The posterior mean, $\alpha_k/\alpha_0$, moves from $(0.33, 0.33, 0.33)$ to
$(0.32, 0.18, 0.50)$: close to the raw proportions $(0.31, 0.13, 0.56)$ but pulled slightly
toward uniform, exactly the two fictitious counts per category still present in
$\alpha_0=22$ against $N=16$ real ones.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why is a simplex, rather than K independent numbers, the right object for a categorical parameter?" concepts={["multinomial-distribution"]}>
Because the $\mu_k$ are not independent: they must be non-negative and sum to one, so
raising one probability necessarily lowers the others. A simplex is exactly that
constrained set.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What role does alpha0 play in the Dirichlet posterior?" concepts={["dirichlet-distribution"]}>
It is the total fictitious prior count, $\sum_k \alpha_k$. Larger $\alpha_0$ relative to $N$
means the posterior mean sits closer to the prior's own mean than to the observed
proportions.
</Recall>

<Recall question="How does the Dirichlet posterior update after observing counts m?" concepts={["conjugate-prior"]}>
By addition: $\boldsymbol{\alpha} \to \boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{m}$, the same
fictitious-count mechanism as the beta-binomial case, one counter per category instead of
two.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the multinomial ML estimate reduce to, and why is that unsurprising?" concepts={["maximum-likelihood"]}>
The observed category proportions $m_k/N$ (2.33): the same result as the Bernoulli case,
because a multinomial trial is just several correlated Bernoulli-like counts sharing one
normalisation constraint.
</Recall>
