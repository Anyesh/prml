## Intuition

Every conjugate prior built so far, the beta for a coin and the Dirichlet for a die, was
found by staring at a specific likelihood until a matching prior fell out. That is not a
coincidence to be rediscovered per distribution: it is one algebraic fact about a shape
almost every named distribution shares, discovered once and then inherited.

Knowing the shape pays twice. It tells you in advance which distributions have a conjugate
prior at all, without a fresh derivation each time. And it tells you exactly which summary
of the data a maximum-likelihood fit depends on, throwing away everything else about the
dataset as irrelevant to the parameters, a claim strong enough to be worth stating in one
place rather than proving distribution by distribution.

## The math

Every member fits one template, $h(x)$ a base measure, $g(\eta)$ a normaliser, $u(x)$ the
statistic $\eta$ pairs with:

<MathBlock id="2.194" name="canonical exponential family form">

$$p(x \mid \boldsymbol{\eta}) = h(x)\,g(\boldsymbol{\eta})\exp\{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{u}(x)\}$$

</MathBlock>

The Bernoulli's natural parameter is the log-odds, $\eta = \ln(\mu/(1-\mu))$, giving

<MathBlock id="2.200" name="Bernoulli in canonical form">

$$p(x\mid\eta) = \sigma(-\eta)\exp(\eta x)$$

</MathBlock>

and the Gaussian's is a pair built from its ordinary mean and variance,

<MathBlock id="2.220" name="Gaussian natural parameters">

$$\boldsymbol{\eta} = \left(\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2},\ -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}$$

</MathBlock>

</Figure>

Differentiating the normalisation condition gives the ML condition directly in terms of a
sum over the data:

<MathBlock id="2.228" name="ML via sufficient statistics">

$$-\nabla \ln g(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{ML}}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{u}(x_n)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Running averages of u(x) for a Gaussian sample settling on their true expectations: the fit only ever needs these two numbers." source="2.11">
  <SufficientStatisticConvergence client:visible />
</Figure>

Every exponential-family member has a matching conjugate prior of the same canonical shape,
with $\nu$ a pseudo-count and $\chi$ a pseudo-observation of $\mathbf{u}(x)$:

<MathBlock id="2.229" name="general conjugate prior">

$$p(\boldsymbol{\eta}\mid\boldsymbol{\chi},\nu) = f(\boldsymbol{\chi},\nu)\,g(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\nu}\exp\{\nu\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\chi}\}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="2.230" name="conjugate posterior">

$$p(\boldsymbol{\eta}\mid X,\boldsymbol{\chi},\nu) \propto g(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{\nu+N}\exp\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\sum_n\mathbf{u}(x_n)+\nu\boldsymbol{\chi}\right)\right\}$$

</MathBlock>

which is the same shape again: the beta and Dirichlet posteriors of 2.1 and 2.2 are this
equation, not an analogy to it.

## Worked example

<Figure caption="A Beta(3,3) prior on µ, redrawn as the general conjugate prior 2.229 over the Bernoulli's natural parameter η, updated by 9 heads and 3 tails." source="2.229">
  <ConjugatePriorFamily client:visible />
</Figure>

Beta(3, 3) on $\mu$ is $\nu=6$ pseudo-observations split evenly. Observing 9 heads and 3
tails adds their sum, 9, to $\nu\chi$, giving exactly Beta(12, 6) after the change of
variables back from $\eta$: the same posterior 2.18 gives directly, reached instead through
2.230's general machinery.

## Recall

<Recall question="What single fact does the exponential family's canonical form buy you?" concepts={["exponential-family"]}>
That conjugacy and closed-form ML are properties of the shape $h(x)g(\eta)\exp(\eta^Tu(x))$
itself, provable once, rather than a private accident of the beta or the Gaussian
individually.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does maximum likelihood in the exponential family depend on, and what does it discard?" concepts={["sufficient-statistics"]}>
Only $\sum_n \mathbf{u}(x_n)$, the sufficient statistic (2.228). Every other detail of the
dataset, its order, its individual values beyond that sum, is irrelevant to the fitted
parameters.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is the general conjugate prior 2.229 not a new idea next to the beta and Dirichlet?" concepts={["conjugate-prior"]}>
Because the beta-binomial and Dirichlet-multinomial updates of 2.1 and 2.2 are this equation
with $u(x)$ specialised to the Bernoulli or categorical case: one derivation, several
instances.
</Recall>
