## Intuition

Section 3.6 closed on an uncomfortable observation: real data is recorded in far more
numbers than it actually needs. A photograph of a face is a grid of a million pixel
values, but faces do not vary in a million independent ways; change the lighting or the
expression and most pixels move together, along a much smaller set of shared directions.
A basis fixed before training ever saw the data cannot exploit that, because it does not
know where the data went.

Principal component analysis lets the data choose its own basis. Ask two questions of
the same cloud of points. Which single direction, picking just one, spreads the data out
the most? And which direction, if dropped, would lose the least on rebuilding each point
from what remained? These sound like different goals, one about maximising and one about
minimising, yet they share exactly one answer, reachable with nothing harder than an
eigenvalue problem.

## The math

For data $\mathbf{x}_n$ with mean $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$, the covariance matrix is

<MathBlock id="12.3" name="covariance matrix">

$$\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\mathbf{x}_n-\bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_n-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{\mathrm{T}}$$

</MathBlock>

Maximising the variance $\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{u}_1$ of the data
projected onto a unit vector, by a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint
$\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{u}_1=1$, gives a stationarity condition:

<MathBlock id="12.5" name="stationarity condition">

$$\mathbf{S}\mathbf{u}_1 = \lambda_1 \mathbf{u}_1$$

</MathBlock>

so $\mathbf{u}_1$ is an eigenvector and the captured variance is its own eigenvalue:

<MathBlock id="12.6" name="variance at the optimum">

$$\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{u}_1 = \lambda_1$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The same rotating direction's captured variance and reconstruction error, plotted together: one curve's peak is the other's trough, at the same angle." width="wide">
  <VarianceReconstructionDuality client:visible />
</Figure>

Asking the question the other way, keeping $M$ directions and measuring the squared
error from discarding the rest, gives the same eigenvectors minimising

<MathBlock id="12.15" name="distortion from M discarded directions">

$$J = \sum_{i=M+1}^{D}\mathbf{u}_i^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{u}_i$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="12.17" name="eigenvector condition">

$$\mathbf{S}\mathbf{u}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{u}_i$$

</MathBlock>

so $J$ is minimised by discarding the smallest eigenvalues. Reconstruction from the
kept $M$ reads

<MathBlock id="12.21" name="reconstruction from M components">

$$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_n = \bar{\mathbf{x}} + \sum_{i=1}^{M}\big((\mathbf{x}_n-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{u}_i\big)\mathbf{u}_i$$

</MathBlock>

and rescaling every kept axis by its own eigenvalue whitens the data to unit variance:

<MathBlock id="12.24" name="whitening transform">

$$\mathbf{y}_n = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{x}_n - \bar{\mathbf{x}})$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

Sixty points in three dimensions, generated along two dominant directions plus a faint
third, give a covariance whose eigenvalues are $8.656$, $3.048$, and $0.084$. Keeping
$M=2$ retains $8.656+3.048=11.704$ of the total $11.789$, which is $99.28\%$ of the
variance. Equation 12.15's reconstruction error for that choice comes out to $0.0844$,
matching the discarded eigenvalue exactly, to nine decimal places, since they are the
same number computed two different ways.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why do the maximum-variance and minimum-error formulations of PCA give the same axes?" concepts={["principal-component-analysis"]}>
Because for orthonormal directions the total variance is fixed, so variance captured
and squared error left over always sum to the same constant. Maximising one term
minimises the other, and both are solved by the same eigenvectors of the covariance.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the reconstruction error equal once the eigenvectors are found?" concepts={["reconstruction-error"]}>
The sum of the eigenvalues belonging to the discarded directions. Nothing about the
individual data points enters once the spectrum is known.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can two eigenvalues that are close in the population still produce very different sample eigenvalues?" concepts={["principal-component-analysis"]}>
Nearby eigenvalues make the eigenvectors ill-determined: a small perturbation from
finite-sample noise can rotate freely inside their shared subspace and, more
surprisingly, can pull the sample eigenvalues themselves further apart than the
population values ever were, even though the subspace they span stays stable.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does whitening change about the data, and what does it leave alone?" concepts={["whitening-transform"]}>
It rescales every principal axis to unit variance, so the new coordinates are
uncorrelated with equal spread. It keeps every dimension, so it is a rescaling, not a
reduction.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does a basis fixed before seeing the data fail where PCA succeeds?" concepts={["principal-component-analysis"]}>
A fixed basis cannot know where the data actually varies, so it must cover the whole
ambient space evenly. PCA reads the directions of real variation off the data itself,
and needs far fewer of them.
</Recall>
