## Intuition

Chapter 3's Bayesian treatment stayed in closed form because a Gaussian prior times a
Gaussian likelihood is Gaussian. Section 4.3's likelihood is a product of sigmoids, and
no prior over the weights turns that into anything with a name, so integrating over the
posterior exactly is no longer on the table.

The Laplace approximation is the cheapest way past that: find the single point where
the true density peaks, measure how sharply it falls off there, and report a Gaussian
with that peak as its mean and that sharpness as its precision. No integral is needed to
normalise it, because a Gaussian's own normaliser is already known.

What this throws away is everything about the density except its behaviour at one
point. A density that is skewed, or has a heavier tail on one side, or is not even
unimodal, gets exactly the same treatment: one peak, one curvature, one Gaussian, and
whatever asymmetry existed is gone from the result.

## The math

For $p(z) = f(z)/Z$, the mode $z_0$ is where the density's own slope is zero:

<MathBlock id="4.126" name="mode condition">

$$\left.\frac{df(z)}{dz}\right|_{z=z_0} = 0$$

</MathBlock>

A Taylor expansion of $\ln f$ around $z_0$ has no linear term there, only curvature, which
defines a precision:

<MathBlock id="4.128" name="precision from curvature">

$$A = -\left.\frac{d^2}{dz^2}\ln f(z)\right|_{z=z_0}$$

</MathBlock>

  <ModeVsMeanMismatch client:visible />
</Figure>

In $M$ dimensions the same argument replaces the second derivative with a Hessian and
gives a full covariance:

<MathBlock id="4.134" name="multivariate Laplace approximation">

$$q(\mathbf{z}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{z}_0, \mathbf{A}^{-1})$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="A two-dimensional density built from two skewed marginals, with its Laplace-fitted covariance ellipse." width="wide">
  <Laplace2DContours client:visible />
</Figure>

Because $Z$ never enters the mode or curvature calculation, this also approximates $Z$
itself: $Z \approx f(z_0)(2\pi/A)^{1/2}$, the seed of the BIC criterion in 4.4.1.

## Worked example

For $p(z) \propto \exp(-z^2/2)\,\sigma(20z+4)$, solving 4.126 numerically gives mode
$z_0 \approx 0.077$. The curvature there gives precision $A \approx 2.544$, so the Laplace
fit is $\mathcal{N}(z \mid 0.077,\, 0.393)$.

Integrating $z$ against the true, correctly normalised density gives a mean of about
$0.673$: a gap of roughly $0.595$ from the mode the Laplace approximation reports. The
true density has real mass on the side the Gaussian's mean never reaches, because the
sigmoid factor keeps that side alive far longer than a symmetric fall-off would.

## Recall

<Recall question="What two numbers does the Laplace approximation need, and what does it never need?" concepts={["laplace-approximation"]}>
The mode and the curvature (negative second derivative, or Hessian) at the mode. It
never needs the normalising constant $Z$, since a Gaussian's own normaliser already
exists in closed form.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can the fitted Gaussian's mean sit far from the true density's mean?" concepts={["laplace-approximation"]}>
Because the approximation is built entirely from behaviour at the mode, and the mode of
a skewed density is not its mean. Nothing in the method looks at the rest of the curve.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What must be true of the mode for the Laplace approximation to be well defined?" concepts={["laplace-approximation"]}>
The precision $A$ must be positive, which requires the stationary point to be a genuine
local maximum rather than a minimum or a saddle.
</Recall>
