## Intuition

Section 3.3 kept a full posterior over the weights because a Gaussian prior and a
Gaussian likelihood multiply into another Gaussian, no approximation needed. A sigmoid
likelihood has no such partner, so a genuinely Bayesian logistic regression has nowhere
clean to land, and section 4.4's Laplace approximation gets reached for out of
necessity rather than convenience.

Fitting the mode is the easy part: the same Newton-Raphson iteration from section 4.3
finds it, with a prior's curvature simply added to the Hessian. What is new is after.
With a distribution over the weights instead of one best value, a prediction has to
marginalise that distribution out, and the result differs from plugging in the
single best-fit weight vector.

That difference is smallest on the boundary and largest for confident-looking points
far from any training data: exactly the reading a point estimate can never give, of how
much a confident answer is support and how much is a model never asked about that part
of the input space.

## The math

With a Gaussian prior, the Newton-Raphson step from 4.3 gains a prior-precision term,
and its Hessian at the mode becomes the posterior's own precision:

<MathBlock id="4.143" name="posterior precision">

$$\mathbf{S}_N^{-1} = \mathbf{S}_0^{-1} + \sum_{n=1}^N y_n(1-y_n)\boldsymbol{\phi}_n\boldsymbol{\phi}_n^{\mathrm{T}}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="4.144" name="Gaussian posterior approximation">

$$q(\mathbf{w}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{MAP}}, \mathbf{S}_N)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The Laplace posterior in weight space: contours of q(w), with wMAP marked." width="wide">
  <PosteriorContourVsMAP client:visible />
</Figure>

Predicting requires marginalising $\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi})$ over $q(\mathbf{w})$,
which reduces to convolving a sigmoid with a 1-D Gaussian over the activation
$a=\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}$. That convolution has no closed form, but the same
probit similarity from section 4.3 does:

<MathBlock id="4.153" name="logistic-Gaussian convolution">

$$\int \sigma(a)\,\mathcal{N}(a \mid \mu, \sigma^2)\, da \approx \sigma\big(\kappa(\sigma^2)\,\mu\big)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The same sigmoid-probit closeness from 4.3, which is exactly what makes 4.153 available in closed form." width="wide">
  <ProbitVsLogisticSigmoid client:visible />
</Figure>

with $\kappa(\sigma^2) = (1+\pi\sigma^2/8)^{-1/2}$ shrinking toward zero as the activation's
variance grows, pulling the prediction toward $0.5$:

<Figure caption="κ(σ²) against σ². Wide activation variance flattens the sigmoid's argument almost to nothing." width="wide">
  <KappaShrinkage client:visible />
</Figure>

Applied to the activation's own mean and variance under $q(\mathbf{w})$, this gives the
predictive probability:

<MathBlock id="4.155" name="predictive probability">

$$p(C_1\mid\boldsymbol{\phi},\mathbf{t}) = \sigma\big(\kappa(\sigma_a^2)\,\mu_a\big)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="A one-feature slice through the same idea: the marginalised curve sits closer to 0.5 than the point estimate everywhere it has not seen data." width="wide">
  <PredictiveShrinkage1D client:visible />
</Figure>

That last fact is 4.155's quiet consequence: $\kappa$ rescales $\mu_a$ but never flips its
sign, so the marginalised decision boundary is identical to the point estimate's. Only
how confident the model is away from that boundary changes.

## Worked example

Take a posterior with $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = (0, 2)$ and $\mathbf{S}_N = 0.5\,\mathbf{I}$, and a
query $\boldsymbol{\phi} = (1, 1)$. The activation moments are $\mu_a = 2$ and
$\sigma_a^2 = \boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi} = 1$.

Equation 4.154 gives $\kappa(1) \approx 0.847$, so 4.155 gives a predictive probability of
$\sigma(0.847 \times 2) \approx 0.845$. The point estimate $\sigma(\mu_a) = \sigma(2) \approx 0.881$
is noticeably more confident: marginalising the posterior pulled the answer toward $0.5$
by about $0.036$, purely from carrying the uncertainty in $\mathbf{w}$ forward.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why is a genuinely Bayesian treatment of logistic regression harder than for linear regression?" concepts={["bayesian-logistic-regression"]}>
The sigmoid likelihood has no conjugate prior, so the posterior is not Gaussian and
cannot be written down exactly. A linear-Gaussian model never hits this problem.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What plays the role of the posterior's precision in 4.143, and where does it come from?" concepts={["bayesian-logistic-regression"]}>
The Hessian of the negative log posterior at wMAP: the prior's own precision plus the
same weighted sum of outer products that section 4.3's IRLS Hessian used, evaluated at
the mode instead of at an arbitrary iterate.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Does marginalising the posterior ever move the decision boundary itself?" concepts={["predictive-distribution"]}>
No. $\kappa(\sigma_a^2)$ only rescales $\mu_a$, and $\sigma(0) = 0.5$ regardless of scaling, so
the boundary where $\mu_a = 0$ is exactly where the point estimate puts it.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Where does the marginalised predictive probability disagree most with the point estimate?" concepts={["predictive-distribution"]}>
Far from the training data, where $\sigma_a^2$ is largest because the posterior is least
constrained there, and $\kappa$ shrinks hardest.
</Recall>
