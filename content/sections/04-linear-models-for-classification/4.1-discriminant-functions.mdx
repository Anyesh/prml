## Intuition

A classifier needs a rule that sends an input to one of K labels, and the simplest such
rule cuts the input space with a straight line: whichever side of a hyperplane a point
lands on decides its class. Restricting to lines and planes is the same trade chapter 3
made restricting regression to weighted sums of fixed features.

The trouble is the target. Regression predicts a real number, so squared error is a fair
stand-in for "how wrong was I". A class label is not a real number, and treating one as
if it were, by regressing onto a 0/1 indicator, quietly imports a Gaussian noise
assumption a label never had.

This section fits a straight boundary three ways without that fiction: least squares
anyway, despite the mismatch; Fisher's projection, which asks only for separation, not
a fitted number; and the perceptron, reacting to mistakes one at a time and never
touching squared error at all. One breaks in a visible way; the other two do not.

## The math

Regress a target matrix `T` of one-of-K rows against the design matrix `X` and least
squares gives the same closed form as chapter 3:

<MathBlock id="4.16" name="least-squares weights">

$$\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = \left(\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{X}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{T}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="A single feature regressed onto a 0/1 label. The fitted line answers a question no probability ever asks: what happens past 1?" width="wide">
  <LeastSquaresRegressionOnIndicator client:visible />
</Figure>

Nothing here keeps a class score inside $(0, 1)$, and a point deep inside its own class
still contributes a large residual against the indicator, one the fit tries to shrink even
though the point was never at risk. Fisher's approach never regresses onto anything: it
projects each point to a scalar $y = \mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}$ and picks $\mathbf{w}$ to
maximise separation against within-class spread, summed directly from the data:

<MathBlock id="4.28" name="within-class covariance">

$$\mathbf{S}_W = \sum_{n \in C_1}(\mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{m}_1)(\mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{m}_1)^{\mathrm{T}} + \sum_{n \in C_2}(\mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{m}_2)(\mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{m}_2)^{\mathrm{T}}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="4.30" name="Fisher's linear discriminant">

$$\mathbf{w} \propto \mathbf{S}_W^{-1}(\mathbf{m}_2 - \mathbf{m}_1)$$

</MathBlock>

The perceptron takes a third route: no fitted score at all, just a rule that moves the
boundary toward whichever point it just got wrong.

<MathBlock id="4.55" name="perceptron update">

$$\mathbf{w}^{(\tau+1)} = \mathbf{w}^{(\tau)} + \eta\,\boldsymbol{\phi}_n t_n$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Step through the perceptron visiting each point once per pass. The boundary only moves on the point ringed in red, and only when that point is wrong." source="4.7" width="wide">
  <PerceptronConvergence client:visible />
</Figure>

None of the three needs more than two classes to define, but building a K-class rule out
of two-class pieces is its own trap: a one-versus-rest or one-versus-one decomposition
can leave a region claimed by none of its binary classifiers, or by more than one.

<Figure caption="Both decompositions on the same three classes. Red marks the region no consistent rule can settle." source="4.2-4.3" width="wide">
  <OneVsRestAmbiguity client:visible />
</Figure>

## Worked example

Three points each: class 1 at $(1,2), (2,1), (2,3)$, class 2 at $(5,6), (6,5), (6,7)$.
Means are $\mathbf{m}_1 = (1.667, 2)$ and $\mathbf{m}_2 = (5.667, 6)$. Each class has one
coordinate with no internal spread and one with spread 2 either way, so equation 4.28
gives a diagonal $\mathbf{S}_W = \mathrm{diag}(1.333,\, 4)$.

Equation 4.30 needs no inversion beyond a diagonal one: $\mathbf{w} \propto (3, 1)$, after
clearing the common factor. Projecting every point onto that direction gives class 1 at
$5, 7, 9$ and class 2 at $21, 23, 25$: a clean gap of 12, against a gap of under 3 if the
raw mean-difference direction $(4, 4)$ is used instead, because that direction spends
equally on the coordinate with 4 units of spread and the coordinate with almost none.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does least-squares regression onto class indicators break, when the same closed form works fine in chapter 3?" concepts={["least-squares-classification"]}>
Because it fits a number, not a label. A point deep inside its own class still produces a
residual against the indicator, and squared error keeps trying to shrink that residual
even when the point was never in danger of being misclassified.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does Fisher's direction reduce to when the within-class covariance is isotropic?" concepts={["fishers-linear-discriminant"]}>
The plain mean-difference direction, $\mathbf{m}_2 - \mathbf{m}_1$. Equation 4.30 only
rotates away from that when the classes have unequal spread along different axes.
</Recall>

<Recall question="When does the perceptron update in 4.55 fire?" concepts={["perceptron-algorithm"]}>
Only on a misclassified pattern, where $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}_n t_n \le 0$. A
correctly classified point leaves the weights untouched.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can one-versus-rest leave part of the input space undecided?" concepts={["multiclass-decomposition-ambiguity"]}>
Each of its K binary classifiers is trained independently, so a point can be claimed by
none of them or by more than one. A single K-output linear model has no such gap.
</Recall>
