## Intuition

Section 4.2 arrived at a sigmoid of a linear function by fitting densities and letting
Bayes' theorem produce it. This section fits that same functional form directly, with
none of the density-fitting in between: far fewer parameters, and no assumption about
what the class-conditional densities look like, because the model never claims to know.

The cost is the closed form. Chapter 3's regression had one because a Gaussian
likelihood is quadratic in the weights; a sigmoid's likelihood is not, so there is no
formula to jump to, only a direction to climb the likelihood in. That direction turns out
to have a clean interpretation: it moves each weight by the error between prediction and
target, weighted by the input, so a large confident mistake pulls harder than a small
uncertain one. Repeating that climb a few times over is the whole method.

## The math

The likelihood of $\mathbf{w}$ under $N$ independent Bernoulli labels gives, after taking a
negative log, the cross-entropy error:

<MathBlock id="4.90" name="cross-entropy error">

$$E(\mathbf{w}) = -\sum_{n=1}^N \{t_n \ln y_n + (1-t_n)\ln(1-y_n)\}$$

</MathBlock>

Its gradient is unexpectedly simple: the sigmoid's own derivative cancels, leaving the
same "error times feature" form as sum-of-squares regression had in chapter 3.

<MathBlock id="4.91" name="cross-entropy gradient">

$$\nabla E(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_{n=1}^N (y_n - t_n)\boldsymbol{\phi}_n = \boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{t})$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The gradient's per-point term after fitting: it stays small near the boundary and grows for confident mistakes." width="wide">
  <ResidualInterpretation client:visible />
</Figure>

$E(\mathbf{w})$ is concave, so a Newton step toward its root converges fast, and that step
happens to be a weighted least-squares solve, re-weighted every iteration as the fit
changes:

<MathBlock id="4.99" name="IRLS update">

$$\mathbf{w}^{(\mathrm{new})} = (\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{z}$$

</MathBlock>

Squared error on the same sigmoid output has no such guarantee: it can flatten to a
near-zero gradient without reaching the best fit, wherever the sigmoid has already
saturated on the wrong side of a point.

<Figure caption="Cross-entropy against squared error on σ(wx), for the same six points plus one outlier. One keeps a restoring gradient everywhere; the other goes flat." width="wide">
  <ConvexVsNonconvexErrorSurface client:visible />
</Figure>

A different activation function gives a different discriminative model entirely: the
standard normal CDF in place of the sigmoid is the probit function.

<MathBlock id="4.114" name="probit function">

$$\Phi(a) = \int_{-\infty}^a \mathcal{N}(\theta \mid 0, 1)\, d\theta$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Rescaled to match slopes at the origin, the two are close everywhere except the far tails." source="4.9" width="wide">
  <ProbitVsLogisticSigmoid client:visible />
</Figure>

## Worked example

One feature, two points: $x_1=-1,\,t_1=0$ and $x_2=1,\,t_2=1$, starting from
$\mathbf{w}=(0,0)$. Both predictions start at $y=0.5$, so $\mathbf{R}=0.5\,\mathbf{I}$ scaled by
$0.25$ and 4.91 gives $\nabla E = (0,-1)$. The Hessian $\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$
is $0.5\,\mathbf{I}$, so one Newton step lands at $\mathbf{w}=(0,2)$.

Predictions move to $y_1 \approx 0.119$, $y_2 \approx 0.881$: both now on the correct side
with room to spare. Cross-entropy drops from $2\ln 2 \approx 1.386$ to about $0.254$ in
that single step, which is what a concave error function pointed at a root buys you.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the cross-entropy gradient take the same 'error times feature' form as linear regression's?" concepts={["cross-entropy-error"]}>
The sigmoid's own derivative, $y(1-y)$, cancels against a matching term from the
log-likelihood. It is a property of pairing a sigmoid activation with cross-entropy, not
a coincidence.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does IRLS re-weight, and why every iteration rather than once?" concepts={["iterative-reweighted-least-squares"]}>
The diagonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ with entries $y_n(1-y_n)$, which depends on the current
$\mathbf{w}$. Each Newton step changes $\mathbf{w}$, so $\mathbf{R}$ is stale the moment the step
lands.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does maximum likelihood fail on linearly separable data?" concepts={["logistic-regression"]}>
The error keeps falling as $\|\mathbf{w}\|\to\infty$ and the sigmoid sharpens into a step
function, so no finite weight vector is optimal even though the classes are perfectly
separated.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does logistic regression have that a Gaussian generative classifier does not need to get right?" concepts={["probit-regression"]}>
Nothing about the class-conditional densities: it fits the posterior's shape directly,
at the cost of a form with no closed-form maximiser.
</Recall>
