## Intuition

Section 4.1 fit a boundary directly and never asked what the data looked like on either
side of it. A generative model asks exactly that: fit a density to each class, fit how
common each class is, and let Bayes' theorem turn those into a boundary rather than
searching for one.

This costs more to fit, since a density over the whole feature space is a bigger object
than a single hyperplane, and it throws away nothing: the same fitted densities also say
how typical a new point is, which a boundary alone never can.

The surprise is what comes back out. Two Gaussians with a shared covariance produce
a posterior that is a sigmoid of a linear function of the input, the exact function form
section 4.3 assumes from the outset. Generative and discriminative modelling land on
the same equation from opposite directions here, which is exactly why it is worth doing
both before picking one.

## The math

For classes with priors $p(C_k)$ and densities $p(\mathbf{x}\mid C_k)$, Bayes' theorem gives a
posterior that is always a normalised exponential of "activations" $a_k = \ln p(\mathbf{x}\mid
C_k)p(C_k)$:

<MathBlock id="4.62" name="softmax posterior">

$$p(C_k \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\exp(a_k)}{\sum_j \exp(a_j)}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Three linear activations and the softmax posterior they produce. The winner sharpens toward 1 wherever it clears the others by a wide margin." width="wide">
  <SoftmaxActivations client:visible />
</Figure>

Nothing there is linear yet. Linearity is a consequence of choosing Gaussian densities
with one covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ shared across every class: the quadratic terms in
$\mathbf{x}$ cancel between numerator and denominator, and for two classes the posterior
collapses to a plain sigmoid:

<MathBlock id="4.65" name="posterior as a sigmoid of a linear function">

$$p(C_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x} + w_0)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="4.66" name="shared-covariance weight vector">

$$\mathbf{w} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="4.67" name="shared-covariance bias">

$$w_0 = -\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_2^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_2 + \ln\frac{p(C_1)}{p(C_2)}$$

</MathBlock>

Let each class keep its own covariance instead, and that cancellation never happens: the
boundary is quadratic in $\mathbf{x}$.

<Figure caption="Same two classes, one shared covariance fit and one separate. The linear and quadratic boundaries agree near the middle and diverge everywhere else." width="wide">

</Figure>

## Worked example

Eight points, four per class: class 1 at $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$, class 2 shifted by $(3,3)$.
Both classes have mean $(0.5,0.5)$ and $(3.5,3.5)$ and, by symmetry, identical scatter
$\mathrm{diag}(1,1)$, so the pooled maximum-likelihood covariance from fitting both at once
is $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = 0.25\,\mathbf{I}$, with equal priors of $0.5$.

Equation 4.66 gives $\mathbf{w} = 4(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2) = (-12,-12)$, and 4.67
gives $w_0 = 48$, so the boundary $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x} + w_0 = 0$ reduces to
$x_1 + x_2 = 4$: exactly the perpendicular bisector of the two means, as isotropic,
equal-prior covariance should give.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does a shared covariance produce a linear boundary but separate covariances do not?" concepts={["class-conditional-density"]}>
The quadratic term in each Gaussian's exponent is $\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}$.
With one shared $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ it is identical for every class and cancels when the
posterior is formed; with separate covariances it does not, and survives into the
boundary.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the softmax reduce to when one activation is far larger than the rest?" concepts={["softmax-function"]}>
A near-one-hot vector: the largest class's posterior approaches 1 and every other
approaches 0, which is why it is called a smoothed maximum rather than an average.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does a generative fit give you that a boundary alone cannot?" concepts={["generative-model"]}>
A density over the input itself, so the model can also say how typical a new point is,
not only which side of the boundary it falls on.
</Recall>
