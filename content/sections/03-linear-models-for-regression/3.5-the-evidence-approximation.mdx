## Intuition

Section 3.3 was fully Bayesian about the weights and quietly cheated about everything else.
It treated two quantities as known: how tightly the prior pins weights towards zero, and how
noisy the observations are. Being consistent means priors on those too, and that integral
has no closed form.

The compromise that works: integrate over the weights, which is tractable, then pick the
single pair of precisions maximising what is left.

It sounds like it should over-fit, since the same data is used twice. It does not, and the
reason matters. The weights are integrated out *before* the maximisation, so what is being
maximised is not how well the model can fit but how probable the data was averaged over all
its weights, which already carries the complexity penalty from 3.4.

The by-product is the better result: a count of how many parameters the data actually
determines, as against how many the model has.

## The math

Integrate out the weights only:

<MathBlock id="3.77" name="marginal likelihood integral">

$$p(\mathbf{t} \mid \alpha, \beta) = \int p(\mathbf{t} \mid \mathbf{w}, \beta)\,p(\mathbf{w} \mid \alpha)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}$$

</MathBlock>

Both factors are Gaussian, so this is closed-form. With $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{S}_N^{-1}$:

<MathBlock id="3.86" name="log marginal likelihood">

$$\ln p(\mathbf{t} \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{M}{2}\ln\alpha + \frac{N}{2}\ln\beta - E(\mathbf{m}_N) - \frac{1}{2}\ln|\mathbf{A}| - \frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The competing terms of 3.86 plotted separately. Fit improves without limit; the Occam term worsens without limit; only their sum turns over." width="wide">
  <EvidenceTerms client:visible />
</Figure>

$-\frac{1}{2}\ln|\mathbf{A}|$ is the Occam factor in exact form: the log-volume of the
posterior, charging for every direction the data pinned down. Compute in logs throughout,
because $|\mathbf{A}|$ underflows long before its log does.

Differentiating with respect to $\alpha$ introduces the quantity this section is about, with
$\lambda_i$ the eigenvalues of $\beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$:

<MathBlock id="3.91" name="effective number of parameters">

$$\gamma = \sum_{i}\frac{\lambda_i}{\alpha + \lambda_i}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="One bar per direction. Tighten the prior and the bars collapse towards zero together, taking γ with them." width="wide">
  <EffectiveParameters client:visible />
</Figure>

Each term sits in $[0, 1)$: near one where data dominates that direction, near zero where the
prior does. So $\gamma$ interpolates between $0$ and $M$ and measures complexity as used.

<MathBlock id="3.92" name="alpha re-estimation">

$$\alpha = \frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{m}_N^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{m}_N}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.95" name="beta re-estimation">

$$\frac{1}{\beta} = \frac{1}{N - \gamma}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big\{t_n - \mathbf{m}_N^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n)\big\}^2$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="The evidence as a surface over both hyperparameters, with the fixed point's actual path across it. The iteration is climbing, not evaluating a formula." width="wide">
  <AlphaBetaSurface client:visible />
</Figure>

<Figure caption="γ against the prior precision, with M+1 marked. It approaches the nominal count from below as the prior weakens and collapses towards zero as the prior tightens." width="wide">
  <GammaAgainstAlpha client:visible />
</Figure>

Both implicit, so iterate. One subtlety bites implementations: the $\lambda_i$ scale with
$\beta$, which the iteration is changing. Rescale them each sweep; freezing them converges
to the wrong answer while looking stable. And $N - \gamma$ is the familiar $N - M$ bias
correction applied to the effective count instead of the nominal one.

## Worked example

The book's 25-point sinusoid, degree-9 polynomial, starting from $\alpha = \beta = 1$.

The first sweep forms $\gamma$ from 3.91 and gets a value well below the model's ten
parameters, because 25 points barely constrain the high-order coefficients. That small
$\gamma$ feeds 3.92, and the residual sum in 3.95 is divided by $N - \gamma$ rather than
$N$, inflating the noise estimate relative to the naive one.

Convergence takes tens of sweeps and produces a joint statement: this much prior precision,
this much noise precision, and this many effective parameters. The gap between $\gamma$ and
$M + 1 = 10$ is the interesting part, and it is clearly positive. Ten coefficients; the data
pays for fewer.

The check worth running on any implementation: $\gamma$ must stay in $[0, M]$, since every
summand is in $[0, 1)$. A $\gamma$ outside that means the eigenvalues were not rescaled.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does maximising the evidence over α and β not over-fit?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
The weights are integrated out *before* the maximisation, so what is maximised already
carries the Occam penalty rather than being the best achievable fit.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does γ measure, and what is its range?" concepts={["effective-parameters"]}>
How many parameter directions the data determines rather than the prior. Each term is in
$[0, 1)$, so $\gamma$ lies between $0$ and $M$.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is 3.95 divided by N − γ rather than N − M?" concepts={["effective-parameters"]}>
Only $\gamma$ parameters were actually fitted; the rest were held near zero by the prior and
consumed no degrees of freedom.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What goes wrong if the eigenvalues in 3.91 are computed once and reused?" concepts={["effective-parameters"]}>
They scale with $\beta$, which the iteration changes each sweep. Freezing them converges
smoothly to the wrong answer, with no visible symptom.
</Recall>
