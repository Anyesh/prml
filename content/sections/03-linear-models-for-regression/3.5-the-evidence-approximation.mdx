## Intuition

Section 3.3 was fully Bayesian about the weights and quietly cheated about everything else.
It treated two quantities as known constants: how tightly the prior pins the weights towards
zero, and how noisy the observations are. Neither is known. Being consistent would mean
putting priors on those too and integrating them out along with the weights, and that
integral has no closed form.

The evidence approximation is the compromise that works. Integrate over the weights, which
is tractable, then pick the single pair of precisions that maximises what is left. It is
empirical Bayes: the hyperparameters are fitted to the data rather than marginalised away.

It sounds like it should over-fit, since we are choosing hyperparameters using the same data
we fitted on. It does not, and the reason matters. The weights have already been integrated
out before the maximisation happens. So the quantity being maximised is not "how well can
this model fit" but "how probable was this data under this model averaged over all its
weights", and that quantity, as the previous section showed, already contains a complexity
penalty. Maximising it is therefore maximising a score that has already charged for
flexibility.

The by-product is the more interesting result. The re-estimation produces a number counting
how many of the model's parameters the data actually determines, as opposed to how many the
model has. Fit ten basis functions to data that only supports three directions and that
count will come out near three. It is a measurement of effective complexity, taken from the
training data, and the frequentist treatment offers nothing like it.

## The math

Being consistent would mean integrating the hyperparameters out too. Instead we integrate
only the weights:

<MathBlock id="3.77" name="marginal likelihood integral">

$$p(\mathbf{t} \mid \alpha, \beta) = \int p(\mathbf{t} \mid \mathbf{w}, \beta)\,p(\mathbf{w} \mid \alpha)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}$$

</MathBlock>

Both factors are Gaussian, so the integral is a Gaussian integral and evaluates in closed
form. Taking logs, with $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{S}_N^{-1}$ from 3.54:

<MathBlock id="3.86" name="log marginal likelihood">

$$\ln p(\mathbf{t} \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{M}{2}\ln\alpha + \frac{N}{2}\ln\beta - E(\mathbf{m}_N) - \frac{1}{2}\ln|\mathbf{A}| - \frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi)$$

</MathBlock>

Every term earns its place. The $\frac{M}{2}\ln\alpha$ rewards a confident prior,
$E(\mathbf{m}_N)$ is the penalised fit error, and $-\frac{1}{2}\ln|\mathbf{A}|$ is the Occam
factor from 3.4 in its exact form: the log-volume of the posterior, charging for every
direction the data had to pin down. Compute this in logs throughout, because $|\mathbf{A}|$
for a degree-nine fit underflows to zero long before its logarithm does.

Setting the derivative with respect to $\alpha$ to zero introduces the quantity this section
is really about. With $\lambda_i$ the eigenvalues of $\beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$:

<MathBlock id="3.91" name="effective number of parameters">

$$\gamma = \sum_{i}\frac{\lambda_i}{\alpha + \lambda_i}$$

</MathBlock>

Each term is between zero and one, and it is a ratio of how strongly the data constrains
direction $i$ to how strongly the prior does. Where the data dominates, the term is near one
and the parameter counts. Where the prior dominates, it is near zero and the parameter is
along for the ride. So $\gamma$ interpolates between $0$ and $M$ and measures effective
complexity rather than nominal complexity.

The two re-estimation formulas follow:

<MathBlock id="3.92" name="alpha re-estimation">

$$\alpha = \frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{m}_N^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{m}_N}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.95" name="beta re-estimation">

$$\frac{1}{\beta} = \frac{1}{N - \gamma}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big\{t_n - \mathbf{m}_N^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n)\big\}^2$$

</MathBlock>

Both are implicit: $\gamma$ depends on $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and $\mathbf{m}_N$ depends on
both, so this is a fixed point to iterate rather than a formula to evaluate. One subtlety
bites implementations: the $\lambda_i$ are eigenvalues of
$\beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ and therefore scale with $\beta$,
which the iteration is itself changing. Compute the eigenvalues of
$\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ once and rescale them each sweep; holding
them fixed converges to the wrong answer while looking perfectly stable.

The $N - \gamma$ in 3.95 is worth a second look. The unbiased variance estimate familiar
from elsewhere divides by $N - M$, the number of observations minus the number of
parameters. Here it is minus the *effective* number, which is the same correction applied
honestly.

## Worked example

Take the book's sinusoidal dataset, 25 points, fitted with a degree-9 polynomial, and start
the iteration at $\alpha = \beta = 1$.

The first sweep computes the posterior at those values, forms $\gamma$ from 3.91, and gets a
value well below the model's ten parameters, because the high-order coefficients are barely
constrained by 25 points. That small $\gamma$ feeds 3.92, and since
$\mathbf{m}_N^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{m}_N$ is also small under a tight prior, $\alpha$ moves. At
the same time the residual sum in 3.95 is divided by $N - \gamma$ rather than $N$, which
inflates the noise estimate relative to the naive one, and $\beta$ moves too.

Iterating to convergence takes on the order of tens of sweeps. What converges is a joint
statement: this much prior precision, this much noise precision, and this many effective
parameters. The gap between $\gamma$ and $M + 1 = 10$ is the interesting part, and it is
positive by a clear margin. The model has ten coefficients; the data pays for fewer.

The sanity check worth running on any implementation is that $\gamma$ stays within
$[0, M]$. Each summand in 3.91 is a ratio of a non-negative eigenvalue to itself plus a
positive $\alpha$, so no term can leave $[0, 1)$. A $\gamma$ outside that range means the
eigenvalues were not rescaled by the current $\beta$.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does maximising the evidence with respect to α and β not over-fit, even though it uses the training data?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
Because the weights are integrated out before the maximisation. The quantity being maximised
is the probability of the data averaged over all weight settings, which already carries the
Occam penalty from 3.4, rather than the best achievable fit.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does γ measure, and what is its range?" concepts={["effective-parameters"]}>
The number of parameter directions the data determines rather than the prior. Each term
$\lambda_i/(\alpha + \lambda_i)$ lies in $[0, 1)$, so $\gamma$ lies between $0$ and $M$, and
sits near $M$ only when the data constrains every direction strongly.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is 3.95 divided by N − γ rather than N − M?" concepts={["effective-parameters"]}>
Because only $\gamma$ parameters were actually fitted to the data; the rest were held near
zero by the prior and consumed no degrees of freedom. It is the familiar bias correction
applied to the effective parameter count instead of the nominal one.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What goes wrong if the eigenvalues in 3.91 are computed once and reused across iterations?" concepts={["effective-parameters"]}>
They are eigenvalues of $\beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ and scale
with $\beta$, which the iteration changes each sweep. Freezing them converges smoothly to
the wrong $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$, which is a failure with no visible symptom.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Which term of 3.86 is the Occam factor, and what is it measuring?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
The $-\frac{1}{2}\ln|\mathbf{A}|$ term. It is the log-volume of the posterior, so it charges
the model once for every direction in weight space the data had to narrow down.
</Recall>

over, and on [3.4 Bayesian Model Comparison](/sections/3-4) for why the resulting quantity
is safe to maximise.

It closes the loop 3.1 opened: the regularisation coefficient that section had to guess at,
$\lambda = \alpha/\beta$, is now determined by the data without a validation set.

$\gamma$ returns in chapter 7 as the mechanism behind the relevance vector machine, where
letting each weight have its own $\alpha$ drives most of them to infinity and prunes the
model automatically.

Chapter 10 replaces this approximation with a fully variational treatment, which puts
distributions over $\alpha$ and $\beta$ instead of point estimates, and recovers a bound on
3.77 rather than maximising it directly.
