## Intuition

Section 3.3 marginalised over weights to get predictions. Do it one level up and you can
compare whole models.

The quantity is the probability a model gave to the data you actually saw, before fitting,
averaged over every parameter setting it allowed. Why that punishes complexity is worth
being concrete about.

A distribution over datasets must sum to one. A simple model concentrates that fixed budget
on the narrow range of datasets it can produce. A complex model spreads the same budget far
wider, so even when the observed data sits comfortably inside its range, its share is diluted
across everything else the model could equally have produced.

The complex model is not punished for being wrong. It is punished for being uncommitted. A
model that can fit anything predicted nothing in particular.

One caveat, and not a footnote: this assumes one of your models is true. If all are wrong it
says which is least bad, not whether to trust any.

## The math

The evidence is the likelihood with parameters integrated out:

<MathBlock id="3.68" name="model evidence">

$$p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_i) = \int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M}_i)\,p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathcal{M}_i)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}$$

</MathBlock>

Approximate that crudely. Let the posterior over one parameter be sharply peaked with width
$\Delta w_{\mathrm{posterior}}$, inside a flat prior of width $\Delta w_{\mathrm{prior}}$:

<MathBlock id="3.70" name="Occam factor approximation">

$$p(\mathcal{D}) \simeq p(\mathcal{D} \mid w_{\mathrm{MAP}})\frac{\Delta w_{\mathrm{posterior}}}{\Delta w_{\mathrm{prior}}}$$

</MathBlock>

Best fit, times a ratio of widths: how far the data narrowed your belief relative to how
wide you were prepared to be. It is at most one, and with $M$ parameters its log is
multiplied by $M$. Nobody chose that penalty; it fell out of the integral.

Averaging the log Bayes factor over datasets from the true model gives

<MathBlock id="3.73" name="expected Bayes factor">

$$\int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_1)\ln\frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_2)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{D}$$

</MathBlock>

a Kullback-Leibler divergence, so non-negative. On average the evidence favours the model
that generated the data. On average, not on your dataset.

The peak is the thing to notice. Training error alone would fall monotonically with degree
and recommend the most complex model on the list. The evidence, computed from exactly the
same data, turns over.

## Worked example

Two models for a coin, one dataset: eight heads in ten.

$\mathcal{M}_1$ says fair. No parameters, so its evidence is just
$\binom{10}{8}(0.5)^{10} \approx 0.0439$.

$\mathcal{M}_2$ says the bias is unknown, uniform on $[0, 1]$, so it marginalises:

$$
p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_2) = \int_0^1 \binom{10}{8}\mu^8(1-\mu)^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu = \frac{1}{11} \approx 0.0909
$$

The flexible model wins by a factor of $2.07$. Eight in ten is uncomfortable enough for a
fair coin that the extra parameter pays.

Now change one number. At six heads, $\mathcal{M}_1$ gives $0.205$ while $\mathcal{M}_2$
still gives $1/11$, and the simple model wins by $2.26$. The flexible model was not punished
for fitting badly, since $\mu = 0.6$ was available to it. Its evidence is $1/11$ whatever
happens, which is a flat prior's honest confession: it predicted every result equally.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the evidence penalise complex models without any explicit penalty term?" concepts={["model-evidence", "occam-factor"]}>
Because it is a normalised probability over datasets. A model that can generate a wider
range of datasets must spread the same total probability more thinly, so it assigns less to
any particular one, including the observed one.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What exactly is the Occam factor a ratio of?" concepts={["occam-factor"]}>
The width of the posterior over a parameter to the width of the prior: how far the data
narrowed your belief, relative to how uncommitted you started. It is at most one, and its
log contributes once per parameter.
</Recall>

<Recall question="When does Bayesian model comparison give a misleading answer?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
When none of the candidate models is true, or when the priors do not represent real beliefs.
The framework compares candidates against each other and assumes the truth is among them;
it cannot report that the whole set is wrong. Held-out data is still the outside check.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does 3.73 guarantee, and what does it not?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
It guarantees that the log Bayes factor favours the true model *on average over datasets*,
because it is a KL divergence and therefore non-negative. It says nothing about any single
dataset, which can favour the wrong model.
</Recall>
