## Intuition

The previous section marginalised over weights to get predictions. Do the same thing one
level up and you get a way to compare models.

The quantity is the probability the model assigned to the data you actually observed,
before you fitted anything, averaged over every parameter setting the model considered
possible. That is a strange thing to compute at first, and it is the whole idea, so it is
worth being concrete about why it punishes complexity on its own.

A probability distribution over datasets has to sum to one. A simple model concentrates that
fixed budget on a narrow range of datasets: the ones it can produce. If the observed data
falls in that range, the model assigned it a high probability. A complex model spreads the
same budget across a much wider range, because it can produce many more datasets. Even if
the observed data is comfortably inside that range, the probability assigned to it is
diluted across all the other things the model could equally have produced.

So the complex model is not penalised for being wrong. It is penalised for being
uncommitted. A model that can fit anything is a model that predicted nothing in particular,
and the evidence charges it for that.

This has no free parameter, no held-out set, and no cross-validation loop. It falls out of
the sum rule. What it does require is that you meant your prior, since a prior chosen to be
vague to avoid taking a position will be scored for exactly that vagueness.

There is a caveat the book is careful about, and it is not a footnote. The evidence compares
models under the assumption that one of them is true. If all your models are wrong, the
evidence tells you which is least bad at the one thing it measures, not whether any of them
should be trusted. Held-out data remains the check on the whole framework.

## The math

The evidence for model $\mathcal{M}_i$ is the likelihood with the parameters integrated out:

<MathBlock id="3.68" name="model evidence">

$$p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_i) = \int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M}_i)\,p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathcal{M}_i)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}$$

</MathBlock>

To see where the complexity penalty comes from, approximate that integral crudely. Suppose
the posterior over a single parameter is sharply peaked at $w_{\mathrm{MAP}}$ with width
$\Delta w_{\mathrm{posterior}}$, and the prior is flat over a much wider range
$\Delta w_{\mathrm{prior}}$. Then

<MathBlock id="3.70" name="Occam factor approximation">

$$p(\mathcal{D}) \simeq p(\mathcal{D} \mid w_{\mathrm{MAP}})\frac{\Delta w_{\mathrm{posterior}}}{\Delta w_{\mathrm{prior}}}$$

</MathBlock>

The first factor is the best fit. The second is the Occam factor, and it is a ratio of
widths: how much the data narrowed your belief about this parameter, relative to how wide
you were willing to be beforehand. It is at most one, and taking logs with $M$ parameters
multiplies it by $M$. So the log evidence is best-fit minus a term proportional to the
number of parameters the data had to pin down.

That is where the penalty lives, and it is not a rule anyone added. Nobody chose the
coefficient; it came out of the integral.

Averaging the log Bayes factor over datasets drawn from the true model $\mathcal{M}_1$ gives

<MathBlock id="3.73" name="expected Bayes factor">

$$\int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_1)\ln\frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_2)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{D}$$

</MathBlock>

which is a Kullback-Leibler divergence, and therefore non-negative, and zero only when the
two models are identical. On average, the evidence favours the model that generated the
data. That is a guarantee about the average, not about your particular dataset, which is why
any single comparison can still mislead.

## Worked example

Two models for a coin, one dataset: eight heads in ten flips.

Model $\mathcal{M}_1$ says the coin is fair. It has no parameters, so its evidence is just
the binomial probability of the observation: $\binom{10}{8}(0.5)^{10} \approx 0.0439$.

Model $\mathcal{M}_2$ says the bias $\mu$ is unknown, with a uniform prior on $[0, 1]$. Its
evidence marginalises $\mu$ out:

$$
p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_2)
= \int_0^1 \binom{10}{8}\mu^8(1-\mu)^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu
= \binom{10}{8}B(9, 3)
= \frac{1}{11} \approx 0.0909
$$

So the flexible model wins here, by a Bayes factor of about $2.07$. Eight heads in ten is
uncomfortable enough for a fair coin that the extra parameter pays for itself.

Now change one number. With six heads in ten, $\mathcal{M}_1$ gives $\approx 0.205$ while
$\mathcal{M}_2$ still gives $1/11 \approx 0.0909$, and the simple model wins by a factor of
$2.26$. The flexible model was not punished for fitting badly: it fits at least as well in
both cases, since $\mu = 0.6$ is available to it. It was punished for spreading its
probability across every dataset from zero heads to ten, which costs it exactly the same
amount either way, while the fair-coin model spent everything near five.

That is the Occam factor with actual numbers. Note also that $\mathcal{M}_2$'s evidence is
$1/11$ regardless of the outcome, which is the flat-prior model's honest confession: it
predicted every result equally.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the evidence penalise complex models without any explicit penalty term?" concepts={["model-evidence", "occam-factor"]}>
Because it is a normalised probability over datasets. A model that can generate a wider
range of datasets must spread the same total probability more thinly, so it assigns less to
any particular one, including the observed one.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What exactly is the Occam factor a ratio of?" concepts={["occam-factor"]}>
The width of the posterior over a parameter to the width of the prior: how far the data
narrowed your belief, relative to how uncommitted you started. It is at most one, and its
log contributes once per parameter.
</Recall>

<Recall question="When does Bayesian model comparison give a misleading answer?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
When none of the candidate models is true, or when the priors do not represent real beliefs.
The framework compares candidates against each other and assumes the truth is among them;
it cannot report that the whole set is wrong. Held-out data is still the outside check.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does 3.73 guarantee, and what does it not?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
It guarantees that the log Bayes factor favours the true model *on average over datasets*,
because it is a KL divergence and therefore non-negative. It says nothing about any single
dataset, which can favour the wrong model.
</Recall>

## Connections

Uses exactly the machinery of [3.3 Bayesian Linear Regression](/sections/3-3), applied one
level up: there the integral was over weights to get predictions, here it is over weights to
score a model.

It answers the question [3.2 The Bias-Variance Decomposition](/sections/3-2) could pose but
not resolve, and it does so from a single dataset.

[3.5 The Evidence Approximation](/sections/3-5) makes it practical by using the same
quantity to choose the continuous hyperparameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$, rather than to
choose between a discrete list of models.

The Occam factor argument carries into chapter 10, where variational methods compute a bound
on this same evidence when the integral has no closed form.
