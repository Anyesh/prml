## Intuition

Section 3.3 marginalised over weights to get predictions. Do it one level up and you can
compare whole models.

The quantity is the probability a model gave to the data you actually saw, before fitting,
averaged over every parameter setting it allowed.

A distribution over datasets must sum to one. A simple model spends that fixed budget on the
narrow range it can produce. A complex model spreads the same budget far wider, so even when
the observed data sits comfortably inside its range, its share is diluted.

<Figure caption="Both curves enclose the same total probability. Drag the observed dataset: where the narrow model covers it at all, it covers it better, because it spent nothing elsewhere." width="wide">
  <DatasetBudget client:visible />
</Figure>

The complex model is not punished for being wrong. It is punished for being uncommitted.

One caveat, and not a footnote: this assumes one of your models is true. If all are wrong it
says which is least bad, not whether to trust any.

## The math

The evidence is the likelihood with parameters integrated out:

<MathBlock id="3.68" name="model evidence">

$$p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_i) = \int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M}_i)\,p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathcal{M}_i)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}$$

</MathBlock>

Approximate that crudely. Let the posterior over one parameter be sharply peaked with width
$\Delta w_{\mathrm{posterior}}$, inside a flat prior of width $\Delta w_{\mathrm{prior}}$:

<MathBlock id="3.70" name="Occam factor approximation">

$$p(\mathcal{D}) \simeq p(\mathcal{D} \mid w_{\mathrm{MAP}})\frac{\Delta w_{\mathrm{posterior}}}{\Delta w_{\mathrm{prior}}}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Widen the prior and the ratio shrinks. An uncommitted prior is charged for its vagueness whether or not the fit is any good.">
  <OccamFactor client:visible />
</Figure>

Best fit, times a ratio of widths: how far the data narrowed your belief relative to how
wide you were prepared to be. It is at most one, and with $M$ parameters its log is
multiplied by $M$. Nobody chose that penalty; it fell out of the integral.

Averaging the log Bayes factor over datasets from the true model gives

<MathBlock id="3.73" name="expected Bayes factor">

$$\int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_1)\ln\frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_2)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{D}$$

</MathBlock>

a Kullback-Leibler divergence, so non-negative. On average the evidence favours the model
that generated the data. On average, not on your dataset.

The peak is the thing to notice. Training error alone would fall monotonically with degree
and recommend the most complex model on the list. The evidence, computed from exactly the
same data, turns over.

## Worked example

Two models for a coin, eight heads in ten. $\mathcal{M}_1$ says fair, so its evidence is
$\binom{10}{8}(0.5)^{10} \approx 0.0439$. $\mathcal{M}_2$ says the bias is unknown, uniform
on $[0, 1]$, so it marginalises:

$$
p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}_2) = \int_0^1 \binom{10}{8}\mu^8(1-\mu)^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu = \frac{1}{11}
$$

<Figure caption="The flat model scores 1/11 whatever happens. Where the curves cross is exactly where the extra parameter starts paying for itself." width="wide">
  <BayesFactorAcrossOutcomes client:visible />
</Figure>

At six heads the fair coin wins instead. The flexible model was not punished for fitting
badly, since $\mu = 0.6$ was available to it. Its evidence is $1/11$ whatever happens, which
is a flat prior's honest confession: it predicted every result equally.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the evidence penalise complexity with no explicit penalty term?" concepts={["model-evidence", "occam-factor"]}>
It is a normalised probability over datasets. A model covering a wider range must spread the
same total more thinly, so it assigns less to whatever actually happened.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What is the Occam factor a ratio of?" concepts={["occam-factor"]}>
Posterior width to prior width: how far the data narrowed a belief, against how uncommitted
you started. At most one, and its log contributes once per parameter.
</Recall>

<Recall question="When does this give a misleading answer?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
When no candidate is true, or the priors are not real beliefs. It ranks candidates against
each other; it cannot report that the whole set is wrong.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does 3.73 guarantee, and what does it not?" concepts={["model-evidence"]}>
That the log Bayes factor favours the true model *on average over datasets*, being a KL
divergence. It says nothing about your single dataset.
</Recall>
