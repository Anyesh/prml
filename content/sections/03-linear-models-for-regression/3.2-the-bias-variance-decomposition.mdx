## Intuition

Over-fitting and under-fitting sound like opposites. They are two terms in one equation, and
any knob improving one worsens the other.

Stop asking how a model does on the dataset in front of you. Ask how it does averaged over
every dataset you might have drawn. Fit the same model to a hundred samples and you get a
hundred curves. Two things can go wrong with that cloud.

It can sit in the wrong place. A constrained model gives nearly the same curve every time,
so the cloud is tight, but if it cannot reach the truth every curve misses the same way.
That is bias.

Or it can spread out. A flexible model gives a different curve per sample, because it is
fitting noise. Individual curves are wild even when their average is right. That is variance.

<Figure caption="Two clouds, same data source. Left: tight and uniformly wrong. Right: scattered, but their average tracks the truth. Neither is good; they fail differently." source="3.5" width="wide">
  <FitCloud client:visible />
</Figure>

Both are set by how much freedom the model has, which is why this is a trade-off. The catch
is severe: computing either needs the ensemble, and you have one dataset.

## The math

The optimal prediction under squared loss is the conditional mean $h(\mathbf{x})$. Expected
loss splits into a part that depends on your choice of function and a part that does not:

<MathBlock id="3.37" name="expected squared loss">

$$\mathbb{E}[L] = \int\{y(\mathbf{x}) - h(\mathbf{x})\}^2p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} + \iint\{h(\mathbf{x}) - t\}^2p(\mathbf{x}, t)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}t$$

</MathBlock>

The second integral has no $y$ in it. It is the noise floor.

<Figure caption="The scatter around the conditional mean is what no model removes. Every error bar in this chapter bottoms out here.">
  <NoiseFloor client:visible />
</Figure>

Average the first term over datasets, expand the square about the ensemble mean, and the
cross term integrates to zero:

<MathBlock id="3.41" name="bias-variance-noise decomposition">

$$\text{expected loss} = (\text{bias})^2 + \text{variance} + \text{noise}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.45" name="ensemble average prediction">

$$\bar{y}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}y^{(l)}(\mathbf{x})$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.46" name="squared bias">

$$(\text{bias})^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\{\bar{y}(\mathbf{x}_n) - h(\mathbf{x}_n)\}^2$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.47" name="variance">

$$\text{variance} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\big\{y^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}_n) - \bar{y}(\mathbf{x}_n)\big\}^2$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Both quantities at a single input. Bias is the gap from the ensemble mean to the truth; variance is the spread of the dots about their own mean. Move the input and watch them trade." width="wide">
  <BiasVarianceAtAPoint client:visible />
</Figure>

The asymmetry matters. Bias compares the *average* fit to the truth and is blind to scatter.
Variance compares fits to their own average and never looks at the truth. So a model can
have zero bias and be useless. The cancelling cross term is also why this is specific to
squared loss.

## Worked example

<Figure caption="Three datasets, two models each. The constants land on top of one another and all miss; the interpolants each chase their own noise." width="wide">
  <ConstantVersusInterpolant client:visible />
</Figure>

The crudest model ignores the input and predicts a constant. Variance is zero, so everything
above the noise floor is bias. Against $h(x) = \sin(2\pi x)$ on $[0, 1]$ the best constant is
$0$, and the squared bias is the mean of $\sin^2(2\pi x)$, namely $1/2$.

The opposite extreme interpolates every point. Bias is near zero, because the noise cancels
when averaged across datasets, but each fit reproduces its own noise: variance is about
$0.04$ at the data points and far worse between them.

Neither is close to optimal, and they need the knob turned in opposite directions.

## Recall

<Recall question="Which term of the decomposition can a modelling choice not affect?" concepts={["bias-variance-tradeoff"]}>
The noise. It is the second integral in 3.37, which contains no $y$: a property of the data
source, not the model.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can a model have essentially zero bias and still be bad?" concepts={["bias-variance-tradeoff", "overfitting"]}>
Bias measures only where the *average* fit sits. An interpolant averages out near the truth
while every individual fit oscillates through the noise.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is this decomposition of limited practical use?" concepts={["bias-variance-tradeoff"]}>
Both 3.46 and 3.47 average over an ensemble of datasets, and you have one. With many, you
would pool them and fit a better model instead.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What happens to bias and variance as regularisation increases?" concepts={["regularisation"]}>
Bias rises, variance falls. Their sum has a minimum somewhere in between.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is the decomposition specific to squared loss?" concepts={["bias-variance-tradeoff"]}>
The split depends on a cross term cancelling when the square is expanded about the ensemble
mean. That is a property of the quadratic.
</Recall>
