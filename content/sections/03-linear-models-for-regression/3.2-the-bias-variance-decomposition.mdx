## Intuition

Over-fitting and under-fitting sound like opposites, and the previous section treated them
that way: too few basis functions and the model cannot reach the truth, too many and it
chases noise. This section shows they are two terms in one equation, and that a knob which
improves one always worsens the other.

The idea is to stop asking how a model does on the dataset in front of you and ask how it
does on average over every dataset you might have drawn. Imagine fitting the same model to
a hundred different samples from the same source. You get a hundred different fitted curves.
Two separate things can go wrong with that cloud of curves.

The cloud can sit in the wrong place. A heavily constrained model produces nearly the same
curve every time, so the cloud is tight, but if the model cannot represent the truth then
every curve in it misses in the same direction. That is bias.

Or the cloud can be spread out. A flexible model produces a very different curve for each
sample, because it is fitting the noise, which differs between samples. Individual curves
are wild even though their average may be close to the truth. That is variance.

There is also a floor. Even a perfect model cannot predict the noise on the next
measurement, and that part of the error belongs to the data, not the model.

The reason this is a trade-off rather than two independent problems is that both are
controlled by the same thing: how much freedom the model has. Constrain it and bias grows
while variance shrinks. Release it and the reverse. Somewhere in between their sum is
smallest.

The catch, and it is severe, is that computing any of this requires the ensemble of
datasets. In practice you have one. If you had a hundred you would not need the
decomposition, you would pool them and fit a better model. So this explains the trade-off
without providing a way to act on it, which is exactly why the next three sections exist.

## The math

The optimal prediction under squared loss is the conditional mean, call it $h(\mathbf{x})$.
Expected loss decomposes into a term that depends on the chosen function and a term that
does not:

<MathBlock id="3.37" name="expected squared loss">

$$\mathbb{E}[L] = \int\{y(\mathbf{x}) - h(\mathbf{x})\}^2p(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} + \iint\{h(\mathbf{x}) - t\}^2p(\mathbf{x}, t)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}t$$

</MathBlock>

The second integral has no $y$ in it, so no amount of modelling touches it. It is the noise
floor.

Now take the first term and average it over datasets $\mathcal{D}$. Add and subtract the
average prediction, expand the square, and the cross term integrates to zero, leaving

<MathBlock id="3.41" name="bias-variance-noise decomposition">

$$\text{expected loss} = (\text{bias})^2 + \text{variance} + \text{noise}$$

</MathBlock>

With $L$ datasets and the ensemble average

<MathBlock id="3.45" name="ensemble average prediction">

$$\bar{y}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}y^{(l)}(\mathbf{x})$$

</MathBlock>

the two model-dependent terms are

<MathBlock id="3.46" name="squared bias">

$$(\text{bias})^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\{\bar{y}(\mathbf{x}_n) - h(\mathbf{x}_n)\}^2$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.47" name="variance">

$$\text{variance} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\big\{y^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}_n) - \bar{y}(\mathbf{x}_n)\big\}^2$$

</MathBlock>

Read the two together and the asymmetry is clear. Bias compares the *average* fit to the
truth, so it is blind to how much individual fits scatter. Variance compares individual
fits to their own average, so it never looks at the truth at all. A model can therefore have
zero bias and be useless, which is what the low-regularisation end of the widget shows.

The cross term vanishing is what makes this exact rather than an approximation, and it is
also why the decomposition is specific to squared loss. Under a different loss the terms do
not separate this cleanly.

## Worked example

Take the crudest possible model: ignore the input entirely and always predict a constant
$c$. Its variance is zero, because every dataset produces the same curve, so the entire
expected loss above the noise floor is bias.

Against $h(x) = \sin(2\pi x)$ on $[0, 1]$, the best such constant is the mean of the sine
over that interval, which is $0$. The squared bias is then the mean of $\sin^2(2\pi x)$,
which is $1/2$. So this model carries a squared bias of $0.5$ and a variance of $0$.

Now the opposite extreme: interpolate, passing exactly through every data point. Bias is
essentially zero, because averaged over many datasets the interpolants' noise cancels and
their average tracks the sine closely. But each individual fit reproduces its own dataset's
noise, so with noise standard deviation $0.2$ the variance at the data points is about
$0.2^2 = 0.04$ per point, and between points it is far worse, because an interpolant through
noisy values oscillates violently in the gaps.

Neither extreme is close to optimal, and neither can be improved by adjusting the same knob
in the same direction: the constant model needs more freedom, the interpolant needs less.
That is the trade-off, and 3.41 says it is not avoidable, only balanced.

## Recall

<Recall question="Which of the three terms in the decomposition can a modelling choice not affect?" concepts={["bias-variance-tradeoff"]}>
The noise term. It is the second integral in 3.37, which contains no $y$ at all: it is the
variance of the target around its own conditional mean, a property of the data source rather
than of the model.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can a model have essentially zero bias and still be a bad model?" concepts={["bias-variance-tradeoff", "overfitting"]}>
Because bias measures only where the *average* fit sits. An interpolant averages out to
roughly the right curve across many datasets while every individual fit oscillates through
the noise, so bias is near zero and variance is enormous.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is this decomposition of limited practical use for model selection?" concepts={["bias-variance-tradeoff"]}>
Both 3.46 and 3.47 are averages over an ensemble of datasets, and in practice there is only
one. If many independent datasets were available, pooling them into a single larger fit
would be the better move anyway.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What happens to bias and to variance as a regularisation coefficient increases?" concepts={["regularisation"]}>
Bias increases and variance decreases. Larger $\lambda$ constrains the weights, so fits vary
less between datasets, but the constrained family is further from the truth. Their sum has a
minimum at an intermediate value.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is the decomposition specific to squared loss?" concepts={["bias-variance-tradeoff"]}>
The separation depends on a cross term integrating to zero when the square is expanded
around the ensemble average. That cancellation is a property of the quadratic, and under
other loss functions the terms do not split apart so cleanly.
</Recall>

## Connections

Follows directly from [3.1 Linear Basis Function Models](/sections/3-1), which introduced
the over-fitting this section decomposes, and from the squared-loss result of chapter 1.

[3.3 Bayesian Linear Regression](/sections/3-3) is the answer to the practical objection
raised above. It avoids the trade-off rather than balancing it, because averaging over the
posterior plays the role the ensemble of datasets plays here, and a posterior is available
from a single dataset.

The observation that an average of high-variance fits is a good fit is the seed of the
ensemble methods in chapter 14.
