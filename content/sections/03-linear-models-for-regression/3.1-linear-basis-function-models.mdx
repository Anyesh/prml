## Intuition

The simplest regression model is a weighted sum of the inputs. It has one fatal property:
it can only draw straight lines, planes, and their higher-dimensional equivalents. Real
data curves.

The fix is smaller than it looks. Keep the weighted sum, but do not sum the raw inputs.
Sum fixed nonlinear functions of them instead. Square the input, take a bump centred
somewhere, take a step: whatever those functions are, the model is still a weighted sum, so
the fitting problem is still linear and still has a closed-form answer. The curvature comes
from the functions, not from the fitting.

That is the whole trick, and it buys a great deal. Everything from this section survives
into Gaussian processes, support vector machines and neural networks, which differ mainly
in where the basis functions come from.

It also has a real cost that the chapter spends the rest of its length addressing. The
basis functions are fixed before any data arrives, so you have to choose how many and what
shape without knowing what you are fitting. Choose too few and no weights can represent the
truth. Choose too many and the fit chases the noise, producing coefficients in the
thousands and a curve that passes through every point while being wrong everywhere between
them. Nothing in the fitting procedure warns you, because from its point of view the second
case is a better fit.

## The math

The starting point is linear in the input variables:

<MathBlock id="3.1" name="linear regression">

$$y(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}) = w_0 + w_1x_1 + \dots + w_Dx_D$$

</MathBlock>

Replacing the inputs with fixed functions of them gives the model this chapter is about:

<MathBlock id="3.3" name="linear basis function model">

$$y(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}) = \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} w_j\phi_j(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$$

</MathBlock>

with $\phi_0(\mathbf{x}) = 1$ so that $w_0$ acts as a bias. The name is about $\mathbf{w}$,
not $\mathbf{x}$: this is a linear model however curved $\phi$ is.

Two common choices. Gaussian bumps, local, controlled by a centre $\mu_j$ and a width $s$:

<MathBlock id="3.4" name="Gaussian basis function">

$$\phi_j(x) = \exp\left\{-\frac{(x - \mu_j)^2}{2s^2}\right\}$$

</MathBlock>

and sigmoids, which saturate rather than decay:

<MathBlock id="3.5" name="sigmoidal basis function">

$$\phi_j(x) = \sigma\left(\frac{x - \mu_j}{s}\right), \qquad \sigma(a) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-a)}$$

</MathBlock>

Polynomials, $\phi_j(x) = x^j$, are the other obvious choice and the one that misbehaves
most instructively, because every basis function is nonzero everywhere: moving one data
point changes the fit across the entire domain.

Assume the target is the model plus Gaussian noise of precision $\beta$. Over an
independent dataset the likelihood is

<MathBlock id="3.10" name="Gaussian likelihood">

$$p(\mathbf{t} \mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{w}, \beta) = \prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{N}\big(t_n \mid \mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n), \beta^{-1}\big)$$

</MathBlock>

Maximising its log is the same as minimising sum-of-squares error, which is where least
squares comes from rather than being assumed. Setting the gradient to zero gives

<MathBlock id="3.15" name="normal equations">

$$\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ML}} = \big(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{t}$$

</MathBlock>

where $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ collects one row per observation:

<MathBlock id="3.16" name="design matrix">

$$\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{nj} = \phi_j(\mathbf{x}_n)$$

</MathBlock>

Write 3.15 as code and it will let you down. The book flags the conditioning issue and it
is not theoretical: for a degree-9 polynomial on 25 points in $[0, 1]$, the condition
number of $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is about $7\times10^{7}$, and forming
$\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ squares it to $5\times10^{15}$, at the
edge of what double precision can represent. Two correct solvers then disagree about the
coefficients by thousands while agreeing about the residual to six digits. The fit is
determined; the coefficients are not. Every implementation here therefore solves the
least-squares problem through an SVD on $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ directly.

The usual remedy for over-fitting is to penalise large weights:

<MathBlock id="3.27" name="regularised error function">

$$\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\{t_n - \mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n)\}^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{w}$$

</MathBlock>

which stays closed-form:

<MathBlock id="3.28" name="regularised solution">

$$\mathbf{w} = \big(\lambda\mathbf{I} + \boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{t}$$

</MathBlock>

That $\lambda\mathbf{I}$ does two jobs at once. It shrinks the weights, and it lifts the
eigenvalues away from zero, so the ill-conditioning above simply stops being a problem. But
it replaces the question "how many basis functions" with "what $\lambda$", and answering it
by trying values against held-out data spends data on model selection. Sections 3.3 to 3.5
are about not having to.

## Worked example

Fit a straight line through three points: $(0, 1)$, $(1, 3)$, $(2, 2)$, with
$\phi(x) = (1, x)^{\mathrm{T}}$. The design matrix and its normal-equation pieces are

$$
\boldsymbol{\Phi} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{t} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Solving gives $w_0 = 1.5$, $w_1 = 0.5$, so the fit is $y = 1.5 + 0.5x$. Residuals are
$-0.5$, $1.0$, $-0.5$, and they sum to zero, which they must whenever the basis contains a
constant term: the first normal equation *is* the statement that residuals sum to zero.

Now add a quadratic term, $\phi(x) = (1, x, x^2)^{\mathrm{T}}$. Three basis functions, three
points, and $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ becomes square and invertible, so the fit passes exactly
through all three and the error is zero. It is also the worst model of the two: it has spent
every degree of freedom reproducing the data, including its noise, and has nothing left over
to say anything about a fourth point. Sum-of-squares error rates it as perfect, which is
precisely the failure mode the next section quantifies.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why is a model with Gaussian basis functions still called linear?" concepts={["basis-function"]}>
Because linearity refers to the parameters, not the inputs. The prediction is
$\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$, which is a linear function of
$\mathbf{w}$ however nonlinear $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ is, and that is what keeps the fitting
problem closed-form.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does solving the normal equations directly get wrong, and when?" concepts={["maximum-likelihood", "design-matrix"]}>
Forming $\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ squares the condition number. For
a degree-9 polynomial on 25 points that turns $7\times10^{7}$ into $5\times10^{15}$, so the
coefficients are no longer determined at double precision even though the fitted curve is.
Solve the least-squares problem on $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ directly, through an SVD.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What two separate things does the λI term in the regularised solution do?" concepts={["regularisation"]}>
It shrinks the weights, which is the statistical purpose, and it lifts the eigenvalues of
$\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ away from zero, which removes the
numerical problem as a side effect.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does minimising sum-of-squares error follow from a Gaussian noise assumption rather than being an arbitrary choice?" concepts={["maximum-likelihood"]}>
The log of the Gaussian likelihood in 3.10 is a negative constant times the sum of squared
residuals, plus terms independent of $\mathbf{w}$. Maximising the one is therefore exactly
minimising the other, so the loss is a consequence of the noise model.
</Recall>
