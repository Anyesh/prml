## Intuition

This chapter has been a good deal for one page of algebra. Fix some nonlinear functions,
stay linear in the weights, and you get closed-form fitting, a closed-form posterior, error
bars, and model selection without held-out data.

The reason not to stop here is in the first line of the deal. The functions are fixed before
the data arrives. In one dimension that is a mild inconvenience. In ten it is fatal.

The problem is coverage. Local bumps work by having one near wherever you want to predict.
Tiling a line takes a handful. Tiling a square takes that squared. Tiling a ten-dimensional
cube takes it to the tenth power. And you must lay them out before seeing the data, so you
cannot put them where the data is.

Two properties of real data rescue this. Data clusters near a lower-dimensional surface, and
targets depend on only a few directions along it. Both are useless to a fixed basis, because
exploiting either needs knowing where the data went.

## The math

No new equations here; the point is a counting argument about the ones already established.

Take Gaussian bumps of width $s$ in $D$ dimensions, wanting one within $s$ of any point in
the unit cube. Along one axis that needs about $1/s$. Covering the cube needs the product
over all axes, so $M \sim (1/s)^D$. With $s = 0.2$ and $D = 10$ that is $5^{10}$, about ten
million basis functions, and $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ from 3.16 would have ten million columns.

<Figure caption="Shrink the spacing and watch the count on the line, then on the square, then against dimension. The multiplication is what gets raised to the power of D." width="wide">
  <CoverageExplosion client:visible />
</Figure>

Every result in this chapter still holds at that size and none is any use. $\mathbf{S}_N$
from 3.54 is $M \times M$ and its Cholesky costs $O(M^3)$; 3.86 needs $\ln|\mathbf{A}|$ on
the same matrix. The mathematics does not break. The arithmetic becomes impossible.

Polynomials are differently bad: a full degree-$d$ basis has $\binom{D + d}{d}$ terms, and
being global, every coefficient affects every prediction.

Note what $\gamma$ from 3.91 would say. With ten million basis functions and a thousand
points, $\gamma$ cannot exceed the number of observations. The evidence framework correctly
reports that almost none of the model is determined by the data, and offers no way out,
because the basis was already chosen.

## Worked example

Handwritten digit images, the running example of chapter 12: 28 by 28 greyscale, so
$D = 784$.

A fixed Gaussian basis covering that space is not merely large. Even two functions per axis
gives $2^{784}$, a number with 236 digits, against perhaps $10^{80}$ atoms in the observable
universe. No computer and no dataset makes this meaningful.

Yet the problem is tractable in practice, and the reason is the first rescue above. Digit
images do not fill that space. They lie near a surface of maybe ten or twenty intrinsic
dimensions, parameterised by stroke thickness, slant, and loop size. Almost every point in
the 784-dimensional cube is noise, not a digit.

<Figure caption="Toggle between spreading centres over the ambient square and placing them along the data. Almost every uniformly placed centre sits where no data ever appears." width="wide">
  <ManifoldInAmbientSpace client:visible />
</Figure>

So what is needed is not $2^{784}$ functions but enough to cover a twenty-dimensional
manifold, sitting *on* it. Which you cannot arrange in advance, because the manifold is only
visible in the data.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the number of fixed basis functions grow exponentially with input dimension?" concepts={["curse-of-dimensionality", "basis-function"]}>
Because local basis functions work by covering the space, and covering needs roughly $1/s$
functions along each axis independently. Over $D$ axes the counts multiply, giving
$M \sim (1/s)^D$.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What two properties of real data make the problem tractable in practice?" concepts={["data-manifold"]}>
Input variables are correlated, so data lies near a manifold of much lower intrinsic
dimension than the input space; and targets typically depend on only a few directions within
that manifold.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can a fixed basis not exploit either of those properties?" concepts={["basis-function", "data-manifold"]}>
Because both require knowing where the data lies, and the basis must be chosen before the
data is observed. Exploiting them means either placing basis functions at observed data
points, as kernel methods do, or learning them, as neural networks do.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What would the effective number of parameters report for a hugely over-specified fixed basis?" concepts={["curse-of-dimensionality"]}>
A value far below $M$, bounded above by roughly the number of observations. The evidence
framework correctly diagnoses that the data determines almost none of the model, but cannot
fix it, because the basis was already fixed.
</Recall>
