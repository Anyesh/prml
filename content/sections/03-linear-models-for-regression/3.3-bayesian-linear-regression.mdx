## Intuition

Least squares hands back a single set of weights and no opinion about them. Fit a
ninth-order polynomial to ten noisy points and it will return coefficients in the
thousands, draw a curve through every point, and offer no signal at all that anything has
gone wrong. The usual patch is to hold out a validation set and tune a regularisation
coefficient against it, which works, but it spends data on model selection and it still
produces one curve with no error bars.

The Bayesian treatment changes what the answer is. Instead of asking which weights are
best, we start from a distribution over weights representing what we believe before seeing
anything, and we ask how each observation reshapes it. The answer is another distribution:
narrower where the data is informative, still broad where it is not.

Two things follow that the point estimate cannot give you. Predictions come with a variance
that widens away from the data, so the model says where it is guessing. And over-fitting
stops being a thing you have to police, because a model with too many parameters spreads
its prior thin and is penalised automatically, without a validation set and without a knob
to tune.

The catch is that this only stays tractable because of a coincidence: the likelihood is
Gaussian in the weights, so if the prior is Gaussian too, the posterior is Gaussian, and
everything stays in closed form forever. That is conjugacy, and it is why this section is
arithmetic rather than integration.

## The math

Take the noise precision $\beta$ as known for now. The likelihood is the exponential of a
quadratic in $\mathbf{w}$, so the conjugate prior is Gaussian, and the posterior is
Gaussian as well:

<MathBlock id="3.49" name="weight posterior">

$$p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{t}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{m}_N, \mathbf{S}_N)$$

</MathBlock>

For the rest of the chapter we use a zero-mean isotropic prior with a single precision
$\alpha$, which is the simplest thing that still shows the behaviour:

<MathBlock id="3.52" name="isotropic Gaussian prior">

$$p(\mathbf{w} \mid \alpha) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{0}, \alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I})$$

</MathBlock>

With that prior the posterior parameters are

<MathBlock id="3.53" name="posterior mean">

$$\mathbf{m}_N = \beta\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{t}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.54" name="posterior covariance">

$$\mathbf{S}_N^{-1} = \alpha\mathbf{I} + \beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$$

</MathBlock>

Read 3.54 from right to left and the whole section is in it. Every observation adds
$\beta\,\boldsymbol{\phi}(x_n)\boldsymbol{\phi}(x_n)^{\mathrm{T}}$ to the precision, and
precision only ever increases. Belief never widens as data arrives. It also says the
posterior does not care in what order the points came, and that the posterior after $n$
points is exactly the prior for point $n+1$, which is what makes the update below
sequential rather than a refit.

Two limits are worth holding onto. As $\alpha \to 0$ the prior becomes infinitely broad and
$\mathbf{m}_N$ collapses onto the maximum likelihood solution: least squares is the special
case where you claim to have known nothing. And with $N = 0$ the posterior is the prior,
unchanged.

What we actually want is not the weights but predictions. Marginalising the weights out
gives

<MathBlock id="3.58" name="predictive distribution">

$$p(t \mid \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t}, \alpha, \beta) = \mathcal{N}\big(t \mid \mathbf{m}_N^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}),\ \sigma_N^2(\mathbf{x})\big)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.59" name="predictive variance">

$$\sigma_N^2(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$$

</MathBlock>

The variance has two parts and they behave differently. The first is the noise in the data
itself and it never goes away, however many points arrive. The second is uncertainty about
$\mathbf{w}$, and it shrinks towards zero as $\mathbf{S}_N$ contracts. So the error bars
narrow to a floor of $1/\beta$ and stop, which is the honest answer: more data tells you
more about the function, never about the noise on the next measurement.

Substituting 3.53 into the predictive mean rearranges it into a weighted sum of the
training targets, with weights

<MathBlock id="3.62" name="equivalent kernel">

$$k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \beta\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}')$$

</MathBlock>

This is worth pausing on, because it says a parametric model was secretly doing something
local all along: the prediction at $\mathbf{x}$ is a smoothing of nearby targets. The
weights sum to one over the training inputs but are not constrained to be positive, so
$k$ is a smoother rather than a probability, and it is the doorway to chapter 6, where we
drop the basis functions and work with the kernel directly.

Two things are worth doing deliberately. Add points all clustered near $x = 0$ and watch
what the sampled lines do far from the cluster: they fan out, which is 3.59 with its second
term still large. Then drag $\beta$ down and add the same points again: each observation is
trusted less, so the posterior contracts more slowly, and the prior keeps its grip for
longer.

## Worked example

Take a straight line, $y(x, \mathbf{w}) = w_0 + w_1x$, with $\alpha = 2$ and $\beta = 25$,
which is the setting of the book's figure 3.7. Before any data, the posterior is the prior:
mean $\mathbf{0}$, covariance $0.5\,\mathbf{I}$.

Now observe one point, $x_1 = 0.9$, $t_1 = 0.1$. The design row is
$\boldsymbol{\phi}(0.9) = (1, 0.9)^{\mathrm{T}}$, and 3.54 gives

$$
\mathbf{S}_1^{-1}
= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
+ 25\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0.9 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.9 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 27 & 22.5 \\ 22.5 & 22.25 \end{pmatrix}
$$

That matrix has eigenvalues of about $48.3$ and $0.95$. The large one belongs to the
direction the observation constrained; the small one is barely above the prior's $2$,
because a single point pins down where the line passes but says almost nothing about its
slope. In the widget this is the moment the circular blob becomes a diagonal ridge.

Inverting and applying 3.53 gives $\mathbf{m}_1 \approx (0.045,\ 0.041)^{\mathrm{T}}$,
which is still close to the origin: one observation against a prior of precision 2 does not
move the mean far. Predicting at $x = 0.9$, the predictive variance from 3.59 is
$1/25 + \boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}_1\boldsymbol{\phi} \approx 0.04 + 0.022$,
so about $0.062$. Predicting out at $x = 3$, where nothing was observed, the same
expression gives roughly $0.30$: five times the variance, from the same posterior, purely
because the second term grows away from the data.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the posterior covariance only ever shrink as data arrives?" concepts={["posterior-distribution"]}>
Because 3.54 builds the *precision*, not the covariance, and each observation adds a
positive semi-definite outer product $\beta\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathrm{T}}$
to it. Adding to a precision can never decrease it, so the covariance never grows.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the predictive variance converge to as N grows, and why not zero?" concepts={["predictive-distribution"]}>
It converges to $1/\beta$, the noise precision's reciprocal. The second term of 3.59 is
uncertainty about $\mathbf{w}$ and vanishes with enough data, but the first term is the
noise on the observation itself, which no amount of data removes.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What happens to the Bayesian solution as the prior precision α goes to zero?" concepts={["conjugate-prior"]}>
The prior becomes infinitely broad and $\mathbf{m}_N$ reduces to the maximum likelihood
weights of 3.15. Least squares is the limiting case of claiming to know nothing beforehand.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is the equivalent kernel surprising for a parametric model?" concepts={["equivalent-kernel"]}>
Because it shows the predictive mean is a weighted sum of training targets, with weights
concentrated near the query point. A model defined by global basis functions and a weight
vector turns out to be doing local smoothing, which is what makes the jump to kernel
methods in chapter 6 possible.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can the posterior be updated one observation at a time without refitting?" concepts={["sequential-learning"]}>
Because conjugacy makes the posterior the same family as the prior. The posterior after
$n$ points is a valid prior for point $n + 1$, so 3.49 applies again with the old posterior
in the prior's place, and the result equals a batch fit over all $n + 1$ points.
</Recall>

## Connections

Builds directly on the basis-function models and the design matrix from
[3.1 Linear Basis Function Models](/sections/3-1), and on the Gaussian conditional and
marginal results from chapter 2, which are what make the posterior available in closed form.

It answers the question [3.2 The Bias-Variance Decomposition](/sections/3-2) leaves open.
That decomposition explains the over-fitting trade-off but needs an ensemble of datasets to
evaluate, so it cannot guide model choice from one dataset. The Bayesian treatment can.

Leads into [3.4 Bayesian Model Comparison](/sections/3-4), which compares whole models by
the same marginalisation used here on weights, and
[3.5 The Evidence Approximation](/sections/3-5), which stops treating $\alpha$ and $\beta$
as known and infers them from the data too.

The equivalent kernel is the thread to pull for chapter 6: once predictions are a kernel
smoothing of targets, the basis functions can be dropped entirely.
