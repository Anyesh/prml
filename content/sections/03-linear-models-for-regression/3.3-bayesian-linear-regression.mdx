## Intuition

Least squares hands back one set of weights and no opinion about them. Fit a ninth-order
polynomial to ten noisy points and it returns coefficients in the thousands, draws a curve
through every point, and gives no signal that anything went wrong.

The Bayesian version changes what the answer *is*. Start with a distribution over weights
and ask how each observation reshapes it. The answer is another distribution: narrow where
the data is informative, still wide where it is not.

Two things follow that a point estimate cannot give you. Predictions carry a variance that
widens away from the data, so the model says where it is guessing. And over-fitting stops
needing policing, because a model with too many parameters spreads its prior thin and pays
for it automatically.

This stays tractable only because of a coincidence: a Gaussian likelihood times a Gaussian
prior is Gaussian. That is conjugacy, and it is why this section is arithmetic rather than
integration.

## The math

Take the noise precision $\beta$ as known. A Gaussian likelihood times a Gaussian prior
leaves the posterior Gaussian:

<MathBlock id="3.49" name="weight posterior">

$$p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{t}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{m}_N, \mathbf{S}_N)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Same axes, same colour scale. A handful of observations is all it takes to pull a flat prior into a tight blob around the true weights." source="3.7" width="wide">
  <PriorToPosterior client:visible />
</Figure>

With the zero-mean isotropic prior of precision $\alpha$,

<MathBlock id="3.52" name="isotropic Gaussian prior">

$$p(\mathbf{w} \mid \alpha) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{0}, \alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I})$$

</MathBlock>

the posterior parameters are

<MathBlock id="3.53" name="posterior mean">

$$\mathbf{m}_N = \beta\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{t}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.54" name="posterior covariance">

$$\mathbf{S}_N^{-1} = \alpha\mathbf{I} + \beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Every ellipse is the 95% contour after one more observation, all overlaid. They only ever nest inward, because 3.54 builds a precision and precision only adds." width="wide">
  <PrecisionAccumulates client:visible />
</Figure>

The whole section is in 3.54. It builds the *precision*, and every observation adds a
positive term to it, so belief never widens. Order does not matter, and the posterior after
$n$ points is the prior for point $n+1$. As $\alpha \to 0$ it collapses to least squares:
that is the special case where you claim to have known nothing.

Marginalising the weights out gives the prediction:

<MathBlock id="3.58" name="predictive distribution">

$$p(t \mid \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t}, \alpha, \beta) = \mathcal{N}\big(t \mid \mathbf{m}_N^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}),\ \sigma_N^2(\mathbf{x})\big)$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="3.59" name="predictive variance">

$$\sigma_N^2(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$$

</MathBlock>

Two terms behaving differently: noise in the data, which never goes away, and uncertainty
about $\mathbf{w}$, which shrinks to nothing. Error bars narrow to a floor of $1/\beta$ and
stop. More data tells you about the function, never about the noise on the next measurement.

<Figure
  caption="Reveal points one at a time. The band tightens where they land and stays wide where they do not, and it stops tightening at the noise floor rather than collapsing."
  source="3.8"
  width="wide"
>
  <PredictiveDistribution client:visible />
</Figure>

Rearranging the predictive mean turns it into a weighted sum of training targets:

<MathBlock id="3.62" name="equivalent kernel">

$$k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \beta\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}')$$

</MathBlock>

A model built from global basis functions was doing local smoothing all along. The weights
sum to one but can go negative, so this is a smoother, not a probability, and it is the
doorway to chapter 6.

<Figure

</Figure>

Two things are worth doing deliberately. Add points all clustered near $x = 0$ and watch
what the sampled lines do far from the cluster: they fan out, which is 3.59 with its second
term still large. Then drag $\beta$ down and add the same points again: each observation is
trusted less, so the posterior contracts more slowly, and the prior keeps its grip for
longer.

## Worked example

A straight line, $\alpha = 2$, $\beta = 25$, the setting of figure 3.7. Before data the
posterior is the prior: mean $\mathbf{0}$, covariance $0.5\,\mathbf{I}$.

Observe one point, $x_1 = 0.9$, $t_1 = 0.1$. With
$\boldsymbol{\phi}(0.9) = (1, 0.9)^{\mathrm{T}}$, equation 3.54 gives

$$
\mathbf{S}_1^{-1}
= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
+ 25\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0.9 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.9 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 27 & 22.5 \\ 22.5 & 22.25 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<Figure caption="One point turns the circular prior into a ridge. The long axis is the direction the observation left undetermined.">
  <PosteriorRidge client:visible />
</Figure>

Its eigenvalues are about $48.3$ and $0.95$. The large one is the direction the point
constrained; the small one is barely above the prior's $2$, because one point fixes where
the line passes but not its slope. That is the blob becoming a ridge in the widget.

Predicting at $x = 0.9$, equation 3.59 gives $0.04 + 0.022 \approx 0.062$. Out at $x = 3$,
where nothing was seen, the same expression gives about $0.30$: five times the variance from
the same posterior, purely because the second term grows away from the data.

## Recall

<Recall question="Why does the posterior covariance only ever shrink as data arrives?" concepts={["posterior-distribution"]}>
Because 3.54 builds the *precision*, and each observation adds a positive semi-definite
outer product to it. Adding to a precision cannot decrease it.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the predictive variance converge to, and why not zero?" concepts={["predictive-distribution"]}>
To $1/\beta$. The second term of 3.59 is uncertainty about $\mathbf{w}$ and vanishes; the
first is noise on the observation itself, which no amount of data removes.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What happens as the prior precision α goes to zero?" concepts={["conjugate-prior"]}>
The prior becomes infinitely broad and $\mathbf{m}_N$ reduces to the maximum likelihood
weights. Least squares is the limit of claiming to know nothing beforehand.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why is the equivalent kernel surprising for a parametric model?" concepts={["equivalent-kernel"]}>
It shows the predictive mean is a weighted sum of training targets, concentrated near the
query point. A model of global basis functions turns out to smooth locally.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can the posterior be updated one point at a time without refitting?" concepts={["sequential-learning"]}>
Conjugacy keeps the posterior in the prior's family, so it serves as the prior for the next
point. The result equals a batch fit over all of them.
</Recall>
