## Intuition

Every model in this book so far has quietly assumed the data points do not know about
each other: shuffle them and nothing changes. That assumption is what makes the
likelihood a simple product, and it is also exactly wrong for a time series. Today's
weather is not drawn independently of yesterday's; a currency does not forget where it
closed last night.

The cheapest fix is to let each observation depend on the one before it and stop there.
That single dependency already buys something real: a run of rainy days makes tomorrow's
rain more likely than the long-run frequency alone would say, which an independence
model can never express no matter how much data it sees.

The expensive fix is to let dependence reach arbitrarily far back without adding a latent
variable. This section shows why that road is a dead end, and points at the one that
is not: pushing the memory into a hidden variable instead of the observations themselves.

## The math

Any joint distribution factorises by the product rule with no assumptions at all:

<MathBlock id="13.1" name="general factorisation by the product rule">

$$p(x_1, \ldots, x_N) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} p(x_n \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1})$$

</MathBlock>

Truncating every conditional to depend on only the previous value gives a first-order
Markov chain:

<MathBlock id="13.2" name="first-order Markov chain joint distribution">

$$p(x_1, \ldots, x_N) = p(x_1)\prod_{n=2}^{N} p(x_n \mid x_{n-1})$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Solid arrows: a first-order chain. Adding the dashed arrows extends it to second order, doubling how far back each node can see." width="wide">
  <ChainGraphs client:visible />
</Figure>

which carries the defining property that the future depends on the past only through the
present:

<MathBlock id="13.3" name="first-order Markov property">

$$p(x_n \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}) = p(x_n \mid x_{n-1})$$

</MathBlock>

An $M$th-order chain reaches further back at a steep price. A first-order chain over $K$
states needs $K(K-1)$ parameters; an $M$th-order one needs $K^{M-1}(K-1)$, exponential in
$M$:

<MathBlock id="13.4" name="second-order Markov chain joint distribution">

$$p(x_1, \ldots, x_N) = p(x_1)p(x_2 \mid x_1)\prod_{n=3}^{N} p(x_n \mid x_{n-1}, x_{n-2})$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Exact parameter counts for K=4, computed for orders 1 through 6: the accent curve is the table this section warns about, the dashed line the linear growth a tapped delay line would give instead.">
  <ParameterGrowth client:visible />
</Figure>

Latent variables sidestep the trade entirely. Give every observation its own latent
$z_n$ and make the $z_n$ a Markov chain instead:

<MathBlock id="13.5" name="latent chain conditional independence">

$$z_{n+1} \perp\!\!\!\perp z_{n-1} \mid z_n$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="13.6" name="state space model joint distribution">

$$p(x_1, \ldots, x_N, z_1, \ldots, z_N) = p(z_1)\left[\prod_{n=2}^{N} p(z_n \mid z_{n-1})\right]\prod_{n=1}^{N} p(x_n \mid z_n)$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Every xn now reaches every other xm through the latent chain, a dependence no finite Markov order on the observations alone could reproduce." width="wide">
  <StateSpaceGraph client:visible />
</Figure>

## Worked example

Five days, coded rain as 0 and sun as 1: R, R, R, S, S. An i.i.d. model fits the
relative frequency of rain, $3/5$, and assigns this exact sequence probability
$(0.6)^3(0.4)^2 \approx 0.0346$ regardless of the order the five days came in.

<Figure caption="The streak sequence RRRSS under each model, same axes.">
  <SequenceProbabilityComparison client:visible />
</Figure>

A first-order chain with $\pi = (0.5, 0.5)$ and persistence $0.7$ on each diagonal
assigns the same sequence probability $\approx 0.0514$, about $49\%$ higher, purely
because it rewards the streak instead of only counting rainy days. Shuffle the same five
labels into S, R, S, R, S and the i.i.d. model gives back the identical $0.0346$; the
Markov model does not, because now it is being asked to price four state changes instead
of one.

## Recall

<Recall question="What does the first-order Markov property actually assert?" concepts={["markov-chain"]}>
That $p(x_n \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}) = p(x_n \mid x_{n-1})$: conditioning on the full
history gives the same answer as conditioning on only the immediately preceding value.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why does raising the Markov order stop being a practical fix quickly?" concepts={["higher-order-markov-chain"]}>
Because the conditional table for an Mth-order chain has $K^{M-1}(K-1)$ entries: it grows
exponentially in $M$, unlike the state space route below, which adds no parameters at
all for reaching arbitrarily far back.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What does the state space model buy that no finite Markov order can?" concepts={["state-space-model"]}>
Every observation becomes dependent on every other through the latent chain, at a fixed
cost per time step, instead of a cost that grows with how far back the dependence must
reach.
</Recall>

<Recall question="What conditional independence makes the latent chain tractable?" concepts={["state-space-model"]}>
$z_{n+1} \perp\!\!\!\perp z_{n-1} \mid z_n$: the latent chain screens off everything on
one side of $z_n$ from everything on the other, which is exactly what a Markov chain
requires of its own variables.
</Recall>
