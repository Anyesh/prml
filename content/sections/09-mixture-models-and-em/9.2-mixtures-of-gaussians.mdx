## Intuition

K-means forces every point to fully belong to one cluster, which is a strange thing to
demand of a point sitting almost exactly between two of them. A Gaussian mixture model
keeps the idea of several clusters but replaces the demand with a question: given several
bell-shaped groups, each with its own centre and spread, how likely is it that each one
produced this particular point?

That question has a graded answer instead of a yes-or-no one, and the answer is what this
section calls a responsibility. A point squarely inside one group is almost entirely that
group's responsibility. A point on the boundary is shared. Fitting the mixture then means
choosing every group's centre, spread, and share of the data so that the observed points,
taken together, were as likely as possible under the model. The catch is that there is no
formula for the best answer, only a way to improve any current guess.

## The math

A mixture places weight $\pi_k$ on each Gaussian component:

<MathBlock id="9.7" name="mixture density">

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)$$

</MathBlock>

The responsibility is the posterior probability that component $k$ produced $\mathbf{x}$:

<MathBlock id="9.13" name="responsibility">

$$\gamma(z_k) = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)}{\sum_j \pi_j\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)}$$

</MathBlock>

Maximising the log-likelihood over the whole dataset has no closed form, because a sum sits
inside the log:

<MathBlock id="9.14" name="mixture log-likelihood">

$$\ln p(\mathbf{X}\mid\boldsymbol\pi,\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \ln\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)\Big]$$

</MathBlock>

It also has a genuine singularity: if $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ lands exactly on a point and that
component's variance shrinks towards zero, that one term diverges:

<MathBlock id="9.15" name="singular likelihood term">

$$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \mathbf{x}_n, \sigma_j^2\mathbf{I}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/2}\sigma_j} \to \infty \text{ as } \sigma_j \to 0$$

</MathBlock>

<Figure caption="Log density at one point as a second, small-weight component's variance shrinks onto it. Nothing else about the model changes; the divergence is real." source="9.7">
  <CovarianceSingularity client:visible />
</Figure>

EM sidesteps direct maximisation: an E-step computes every $\gamma(z_{nk})$ from the current
parameters, then an M-step re-fits each parameter as a responsibility-weighted statistic,
holding $\gamma$ fixed:

<MathBlock id="9.17" name="mean update">

$$\boldsymbol\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n \gamma(z_{nk})\,\mathbf{x}_n$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="9.19" name="covariance update">

$$\boldsymbol\Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n \gamma(z_{nk})(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^{\mathrm{T}}$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="9.22" name="mixing coefficient update">

$$\pi_k = \frac{N_k}{N}, \qquad N_k = \sum_n \gamma(z_{nk})$$

</MathBlock>

## Worked example

Take a two-component mixture with weights $0.6$ and $0.4$, means $(0,0)$ and $(3,3)$, and
the covariances plotted above. At $\mathbf{x}=(1.5,1.5)$, exactly between the two means, the
log density is $-3.94$ and the responsibilities are $\gamma_1 = 0.913$, $\gamma_2 = 0.087$:
closer in Euclidean distance to neither mean, but far more probable under the first
component once each component's own spread and weight are accounted for.

<Figure caption="The two responsibilities at (1.5, 1.5), sized by their value.">
  <ResponsibilityWorked client:visible />
</Figure>

K-means at the same means would give this point entirely to whichever mean is nearer,
losing the fact that the two components disagree far less evenly than a coin flip.

## Recall

<Recall question="What does a responsibility gamma(z_k) actually mean?" concepts={["responsibility"]}>
It is the posterior probability, under the current parameters, that component $k$
generated a given point, computed from Bayes' theorem with $\pi_k$ as the prior. It
replaces K-means's 0/1 indicator with a number between 0 and 1.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Why can the mixture log-likelihood not be maximised in closed form?" concepts={["gaussian-mixture"]}>
The log of a sum over components does not simplify the way the log of a single Gaussian
does; each term in the log-likelihood mixes all $K$ components together, coupling every
parameter to every other.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Is the mixture likelihood's singularity a bug in the fitting procedure?" concepts={["likelihood-singularity"]}>
No. It is a genuine unboundedness of the likelihood function itself: a component that
collapses onto one point with vanishing variance drives that one density term, and hence
the whole likelihood, to infinity. Good implementations detect and avoid it; they cannot
define it away.
</Recall>

<Recall question="In one sentence, what do the E-step and M-step each hold fixed?" concepts={["expectation-maximisation"]}>
The E-step fixes the parameters and updates the responsibilities; the M-step fixes the
responsibilities and updates the parameters against them, as a weighted statistic.
</Recall>
