## Intuition

The previous section derived the E-step and M-step by differentiating the mixture
log-likelihood directly. That derivation worked, but it is a coincidence-looking pile of
algebra unless you can see why it had to come out that way. This section supplies the why.

Imagine someone secretly told you which component generated every point. Fitting the
mixture would then be easy: split the data by its labels and fit each Gaussian separately.
Nobody tells you the labels, so instead you use your current best guess about them, in the
form of a probability over labels for every point, and fit against that guess on average.
That is the entire idea. It also explains a fact from last section that otherwise looks like
a happy accident: fixing every component to share one variance and shrinking that variance
to nothing turns this soft guessing back into K-means's flat commitment.

## The math

Treat every $\gamma(z_{nk})$ as coming from a posterior over an unobserved label, rather
than as something calculus happened to produce:

<MathBlock id="9.39" name="responsibility as an expectation">

$$\mathbb{E}[z_{nk}] = \gamma(z_{nk})$$

</MathBlock>

Plugging that into the log of the complete-data likelihood and taking its expectation gives
the quantity EM actually maximises at each M-step, a stand-in for the log-likelihood you
cannot maximise directly because the labels are unknown:

<MathBlock id="9.30" name="expected complete-data log-likelihood functional">

$$Q(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\theta^{\text{old}}) = \mathbb{E}_{Z}\big[\ln p(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z}\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\big]$$

</MathBlock>

<MathBlock id="9.40" name="Q function for a Gaussian mixture">

$$Q = \sum_{n}\sum_{k}\gamma(z_{nk})\big\{\ln \pi_k + \ln\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)\big\}$$

</MathBlock>

Maximising 9.40 over $\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k,\pi_k$ reproduces last section's
update equations exactly, which is the point: two routes, one answer.

  <CompleteVsIncompleteLL client:visible />
</Figure>

Now share one covariance $\varepsilon\mathbf{I}$ across every component:

<MathBlock id="9.42" name="responsibility under a shared shrinking variance">

$$\gamma(z_{nk}) = \frac{\pi_k \exp\{-\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k\rVert^2/2\varepsilon\}}{\sum_j \pi_j \exp\{-\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_j\rVert^2/2\varepsilon\}}$$

</MathBlock>

As $\varepsilon\to0$ the responsibility for the nearest mean is driven to 1 and every other
one to 0, and $Q$ itself converges to the negative of the K-means distortion measure:

<MathBlock id="9.43" name="K-means as the zero-variance limit">

$$Q \to -\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_n\sum_k r_{nk}\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k\rVert^2 + \text{const}$$

</MathBlock>

</Figure>

## Worked example

Fix two means from the previous section's data. At a point near their boundary, the
responsibility to the farther mean is $0.00106$ at $\varepsilon=1.2$, falls to $4.0\times
10^{-72}$ at $\varepsilon=0.05$, and underflows to exactly $0$ in double precision by
$\varepsilon=0.001$: a soft indicator becoming a hard one is not a metaphor here, it is a
number hitting the floor of floating point.

## Recall

<Recall question="What does the Q function stand in for, and why is it needed?" concepts={["complete-data-log-likelihood"]}>
It stands in for the log-likelihood of the fully-labelled data, which cannot be computed
because the labels are unknown. Replacing each label by its expectation under the current
responsibilities gives something that can be maximised, and maximising it is exactly what
the M-step does.
</Recall>

<Recall question="How does K-means emerge from EM for a Gaussian mixture, precisely?" concepts={["k-means-as-em-limit"]}>
Fix every component's covariance to a shared, non-re-estimated epsilon*I. As epsilon shrinks
to zero, every responsibility becomes a hard 0/1 indicator for the nearest mean, and the
mean-only M-step and the Q function reduce exactly to K-means's update and its distortion
measure.
</Recall>

<Recall question="Is the equivalence between Q's maximisation and last section's update equations a coincidence?" concepts={["expectation-maximisation"]}>
No. Both routes maximise the same underlying quantity; one reaches it by differentiating the
mixture log-likelihood directly, the other by first replacing the unknown labels with their
expectations. Agreement between them is a consistency check, not a coincidence.
</Recall>
